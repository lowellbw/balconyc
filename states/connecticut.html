1Is plug-in solar legal in Connecticut?

Connecticut has signed a plug-in solar law (HB 5340 (Public Act 26-127)) that takes effect on 1 October 2026. Until then, the utility’s interconnection rules still apply.

Connecticut’s law takes effect on 1 October 2026 and asks the customer to file nothing: it has no notice requirement, and neither United Illuminating, Bridgeport’s utility, nor Eversource may require approval, a fee or extra equipment. It allows one device behind each meter, with a maximum output of 1,200 W, plugged into a standard 120 V outlet and not permanently fixed to the structure. The equipment list is long: certified by UL or an equivalent laboratory, meeting UL 1741, IEEE 1547, the National Electrical Code and the State Building Code, with protection against energizing the building in an outage.

The detail that matters most for savings is a required warning. Every device must tell the buyer that generation beyond what the home is using will be charged to the customer as usage, unless the customer has a meter that allows net metering. In testimony reported in March 2026, an Eversource vice president told legislators that the company’s meters have anti-tampering features that would not recognize excess power from unregistered panels. balco.nyc’s savings figures below assume every kWh is used at home; in Connecticut that assumption is also the condition for not paying for your own exports.

The act gives renters and condo owners nothing new. It keeps the State Building Code, the Fire Safety Code and local ordinances in force for these devices, and asks code officials to consider plug-in provisions in their next updates.

Sec. 9 of PA 26-127 (marked 'Effective October 1, 2026') covers a 'portable solar generation device': not permanently affixed, max power output of 1,200 W or less, plugs into a 120 V outlet behind the meter. It is exempt from PURA interconnection requirements, but only one device is allowed behind each meter, and utilities cannot require approval, fees or extra equipment. The device must carry a warning that generation beyond on-site use is charged to the customer as usage unless the customer has a net-metering meter, so exports are not credited by default. The act gives no landlord, tenant or HOA right. It says the device stays subject to the State Building Code, Fire Safety Code and local ordinances. Sec. 10 asks code officials to consider plug-in provisions in their next updates. The statute says 'maximum power output', not specifically AC inverter output. The 2026 regular session ended May 6, 2026. The Oct 1, 2026 date and June 4, 2026 signing in trackers match the act; no conflicts found.

3What it would save, and when it pays back

Connecticut’s average residential price over the twelve months to June 2026 was 28.4¢/kWh, above the US average of 17.9¢, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration. That is an average: it spreads fixed monthly charges across every kWh, so it can differ from what a kWh you no longer buy actually saves. The calculator lets you enter the rate from your own bill instead.

Payback assumes every kWh is used at home, a 3% annual rise in the electricity price and 0.5% a year of panel degradation, with no discounting. With the $1,200 mid-range kit an east- or west-facing balcony pays back in 8.6 years and a north-facing one in 18.0 years. A household using Connecticut’s average of 695 kWh a month would cover about 8% of its electricity with a south-facing panel.

4Emissions

Bridgeport is on EPA’s NPCC New England grid subregion (NEWE), which emitted 0.539 lb of CO₂ per kWh generated in 2023 (eGRID2023). A south-facing panel producing 631 kWh a year would avoid about 341 lb of CO₂.

5Common questions

Is balcony solar legal in Connecticut?

Connecticut has signed a plug-in solar law (HB 5340 (Public Act 26-127)) that takes effect on 1 October 2026. Until then, the utility’s interconnection rules still apply. Public Act 26-127 (HB 5340) sec. 9 exempts one plug-in solar device of up to 1,200 W per meter from interconnection, utility approval and fees. Takes effect Oct 1, 2026.

How much would a balcony solar panel produce in Connecticut?

About 631 kWh a year for an unshaded 800 W system on a south-facing railing in Bridgeport, 448 kWh facing east or west, and 190 kWh facing north, in balco.nyc’s PVWatts model. Shade from buildings or trees opposite lowers it; the calculator models that for your address.

How much would balcony solar save in Connecticut?

About $179 a year for a south-facing panel at Connecticut’s average residential price of 28.4¢/kWh, with a payback of about 6.3 years on a $1,200 kit. Savings assume all the power is used at home.

Does Connecticut pay for surplus balcony solar power?

Surplus power earns nothing: the law excludes these devices from net metering.

Do renters in Connecticut need permission to install balcony solar?

The law gives no right to install. A renter still needs the landlord’s permission, and a condo or co-op resident the board’s.

What happens to surplus balcony solar power in Connecticut?

Unless your meter allows net metering, it can cost you. Public Act 26-127 requires every plug-in device to carry a warning that generation beyond the home's consumption will be charged to the customer as usage unless the customer has a net-metering meter. A system sized below the home's daytime use avoids paying for exports.
