Sizing a system

400, 800 or 1,200 watts: what size balcony solar system makes sense?

For most apartments, 400 or 800 W: the third 400 W, which takes a system to 1,200 W, repays its cost within about 12 years only where the home draws some 300 W all day. balco.nyc’s hourly PVWatts model of a south-facing New York City railing prices each extra 400 W separately: in an apartment drawing a steady 150 W, the first 400 W saves about $89 a year and repays its $600 in 6.3 years, the second saves $35 and takes 14.5, and the third saves $17 and does not repay its cost within the 25 years the calculator models. Size to what your home draws while the sun is up, not to the legal cap.

1Each extra 400 W is worth less than the one before

If you use every kilowatt-hour, size does not change payback: output and kit cost both scale with watts, so in New York City an unshaded south-facing railing of 400, 800 or 1,600 W pays back in the same 5.5 years at Con Edison’s 34¢ marginal rate. balco.nyc’s calculator makes that assumption. It fails for output the apartment is not drawing at that moment, which flows to the grid and, under every signed state plug-in law, earns nothing.

So balco.nyc priced each 400 W step separately. It ran PVWatts V8, from the National Laboratory of the Rockies (formerly NREL), hour by hour for a vertical, south-facing New York City railing with the calculator’s parameters (reproducing the site’s 635.7 kWh a year for 800 W), scaled the 8,760 hours to each size, applied the 0.95 railing factor, and set every hour against an apartment drawing a steady 100 to 300 W. Each step adds 302 kWh a year of output. How much of it gets used:

The draws bracket real homes. NRDC’s 2015 smart-meter study defined idle load as a home’s lowest hourly level: 218 W on average across 70,000 northern California homes, with a quarter of a Bay Area subsample below 119 W, half above 185 W and a quarter above 282 W. The one audited apartment idled at 92 W. At a 150 W floor the third 400 W adds 49 kWh of use and sends 84% of its output to the grid, unpaid (see whether you get paid for surplus).

A whole-system payback hides this. At a 150 W floor a 1,200 W system pays back its $1,800 in 11.3 years, which looks tolerable, but that averages a 6.3-year first step, a 14.5-year second and a third that returns $568 over 25 years on its $600. Averages flatter the last panel.

Lifetime totals pull the other way. Any step that pays back inside 25 years raises the nominal 25-year total net of hardware, so the total peaks at a larger size: 800 W for a 150 W floor ($3,023, against $2,991 at 1,200 W), 1,200 W for 200 W, 1,600 W for 300 W. But the steps that get there take 14.5 to 19 years to break even, count a dollar in year 25 as one today, and assume the hardware lasts. A ceiling, not an expectation. The straight-line cost is an assumption too: a panel that shares an inverter may cost less than $600, which is what the second figure in each cell is for.

3A west-facing railing can carry more watts

Surplus comes from the midday peak, so a lower peak leaves room for more panel. balco.nyc ran the same hourly model for a west-facing railing (reproducing the site’s 443.6 kWh for 800 W). Each 400 W makes 211 kWh a year against 302, and 800 W peaks at 563 W instead of 691 W. At a 150 W floor:

Its output also leans toward summer, 53 kWh in July against 19 in December for 800 W before the railing loss, when air conditioners run; a constant floor ignores that, so for an air-conditioned west-facing apartment these figures are conservative. A south railing is the reverse, producing most in winter (see why a south railing is a winter panel). An east railing makes a similar annual total to the west one, but balco.nyc did not run it hourly, and a building across the street, which also trims the peak, is not in these runs.

4What the laws allow: 1,200 W in most states, 420 W and 1,920 W at the edges

Eight of the nine signed state plug-in laws cap a device at 1,200 W, as do New York’s SUNNY Act, which has passed both chambers and awaits the Governor's signature, and California’s SB 868, with the Governor as of 23 September 2026 (his deadline is 30 September). The caps are written as output: New York’s and California’s bills, Vermont and Maine say inverter or AC outright; Utah, Connecticut and Virginia say only “maximum power output.” The calculator’s sizes are panel watts, and at its DC-to-AC ratio of 1.1 their inverters are about 364, 727, 1,091 and 1,455 W. For an apartment the cap rarely binds: the rule in section 6 reaches 1,200 W only at a 300 W floor. Maine prices the second step explicitly: 400 to 800 W needs a licensed electrician and a dedicated circuit on top of $600 of hardware, for a step worth about $35 a year in the New York example at a 150 W floor. Colorado’s 1,920 W allows 1,600 W of panel, but Colorado’s EIA average residential price is 16.45¢ (12 months to June 2026), under half of 34¢, and Denver’s stronger sun (805 kWh from an 800 W south railing, against New York’s 604) probably means more surplus at the same floor, though balco.nyc did not run Denver hourly.

400 W also sits at a second line. Colorado, Maryland and Virginia exempt devices of 391 W or less from code or listing provisions that would require rewiring, New Jersey’s line is 400 W, and from 1 January 2027 Colorado bars selling devices over 391 W that are not UL labeled and listed. Whether a product falls under a line depends on how each law measures output. Where a cap counts inverter output, more panel behind a capped inverter is a question of wording; it barely changes self-consumption, since clipping removes only output far above any apartment’s floor. Every law side by side has the rest.

5The circuit is the other ceiling

Size also sets how much current the inverter adds to a household circuit. balco.nyc’s safety guide works through the worst case: at 120 V a 400, 800 or 1,200 W inverter adds 3.3, 6.7 or 10 A, and on a shared 15 A circuit, loads beyond the inverter’s outlet can pull that plus the breaker’s 15 A through part of the wire — 18.3, 21.7 or 25 A — without tripping it. If the gap between the 80% continuous-load convention and the breaker’s rating is the only slack, it is 3 A on a 15 A circuit, about 360 W.

In the hourly run, a 400 W south railing peaked at 345 W (2.9 A) and never exceeded 360 W; 800 W did for 455 hours a year, 1,200 W for 944 and 1,600 W for 1,329. Hourly averages smooth over brief peaks and the worst case is set by the inverter’s rating, so read these as exposure, not a safety margin. Maine’s 420 W tier, about 3.5 A, is the one cap near that slack; above it Maine requires a dedicated single-outlet circuit, which removes the worst case at any size. This is not electrical advice: whether a circuit can host a system is a question for a licensed electrician.

6The sizing rule: about 400 W of panel per 100 W of floor

At New York City’s price, allow about 400 W of panel for every 100 W your home draws when nothing is in use, round down to a 400 W step, and stop at your state’s cap. In balco.nyc’s runs, every step inside that line repaid its $600 in 12.2 years or less (13.7 on the west railing), and every step beyond it took 14.5 years or more, or never repaid.

Finding your floor. Con Edison says its smart meters let customers see electric use in 15-minute increments; the lowest level that recurs overnight and while you are out is your floor, close to how NRDC measured it. Without that, NRDC’s idle load was 31% to 36% of annual use in its two climate zones and 29% in the audited apartment, so a third of your average draw is a rough guide: monthly kWh divided by 2.2, in watts. At 350 kWh a month that is about 160 W, which the rule puts at 400 W.

Adjusting it. If someone is home drawing power through the day, size to that daytime draw: a steady 200 W while the sun is up justifies 800 W whatever the overnight floor. Where power is cheaper, shrink the rule. At the US average of 17.9¢ (EIA, 12 months to June 2026), the same runs put the first 400 W at 9.9 to 13.3 years and the second under 20 only with a floor of 200 W or more: roughly halve the rule, which for most apartments means one 400 W step. The sun here is New York’s; other cities will differ in the numbers more than the direction. balco.nyc’s calculator offers all four sizes but assumes every kWh is used, so treat its larger sizes as ceilings.

7Common questions

Is 800 W or 1,200 W balcony solar better?

For most apartments, 800 W or less. In balco.nyc's hourly New York City model, going from 800 to 1,200 W adds about 302 kWh a year of output, but a home drawing a steady 150 W uses only 49 kWh of it, about $17 a year at Con Edison's 34-cent rate, which does not repay the step's $600 within 25 years. The extra 400 W pays back in about 12 years only where the home draws a steady 300 W.

What size balcony solar system do I need?

A rough rule from balco.nyc's hourly model of New York City: about 400 W of panel for every 100 W your home draws when nothing is in use, rounded down to a 400 W step and capped by your state's limit. That gives 400 W for a floor of 100 to 150 W, 800 W for 200 W and 1,200 W for 300 W. Where electricity costs about half New York City's 34 cents, roughly halve it.

Is a 400 W balcony solar system worth it?

It is usually the best value per dollar, because a home can use nearly all of 400 W as it is produced. balco.nyc's hourly New York City model puts payback on $600 of hardware at 5.5 to 7.5 years for homes drawing a steady 100 to 300 W at Con Edison's 34-cent rate, and about 10 to 13 years at the US average price of 17.9 cents.

How many solar panels should I put on my balcony?

For most apartments, 400 to 800 W: one or two panels, if each is rated about 400 W. In balco.nyc's hourly New York City model, a home drawing a steady 150 W uses 262 kWh a year from the first 400 W, 102 from the second and 49 from the third, because every panel peaks at the same midday hours. A third panel pays back within about 12 years only where the home draws 300 W around the clock.

Can I install more than 1,200 W of balcony solar?

In most states with a plug-in law, not as a plug-in device: eight of the nine signed laws cap a device at 1,200 W, and New York's SUNNY Act, which has passed both chambers and awaits the Governor's signature, uses the same figure. Colorado allows 1,920 W. Several laws count inverter output rather than panel watts, so check the wording; this is not legal advice.

Why doesn't a bigger balcony solar system save proportionally more?

Because a home's load does not grow when panels are added, and power not used the moment it is produced goes to the grid, usually unpaid. In balco.nyc's New York City model with a steady 150 W draw, going from 400 to 1,200 W triples output but raises the electricity used at home by only 58%, from 262 to 413 kWh a year.
