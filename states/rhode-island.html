1Is plug-in solar legal in Rhode Island?

A plug-in solar bill in Rhode Island (H 7269 (Sub A) / S 2359) did not become law. The utility’s interconnection rules apply, which usually means approval is required.

H 7269 passed the House 70–0 in June 2026 but never reached a Senate vote, and Rhode Island bills do not carry over, so it would have to be refiled when the General Assembly returns in January 2027. Until then Providence residents deal with Rhode Island Energy, whose interconnection pages leave no room for doubt: customers are “NOT permitted to operate any generation capacity prior to receiving an official Authorization to Interconnect”, and are liable for damage caused by unauthorized operation.

The formal route for a small system is the Simplified Process under the utility’s interconnection tariff (RIPUC No. 2244). It is written for contractors, asking for a one-line diagram, a site diagram and specification sheets for every piece of equipment, and the same application is how a system signs up for net metering — which H 7269 would have closed to plug-in devices altogether.

At 29.0¢/kWh Rhode Island’s average price is the fourth highest in the country, which is why the question matters more here than in most states. balco.nyc’s calculator models a specific Providence address and lets you enter the rate from your own bill.

As introduced (LC003246), H 7269 defines a portable solar generation device as up to 1,200 W on a 120 V outlet, UL/NRTL certified. It removes such devices from 'eligible net-metering system', exempts them from interconnection and bars utility approval/fees, and has no landlord provision. Per trackers: House Corporations held it for study 3/19/26, then recommended Substitute A 6/8/26; the House passed it 70-0 on 6/9/26 and it went to Senate Environment & Agriculture. Senate companion S 2359 was held for further study. The General Assembly ended its 2026 session on the night of June 11-12 and RI bills do not carry over, so it needs reintroduction in 2027. Substitute A text and the vote were not verified on official pages: rilegislature.gov and status.rilegislature.gov were unreachable from this network, so the original text was read via an archive.org snapshot.

3What it would save, and when it pays back

Rhode Island’s average residential price over the twelve months to June 2026 was 29.0¢/kWh, above the US average of 17.9¢, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration. That is an average: it spreads fixed monthly charges across every kWh, so it can differ from what a kWh you no longer buy actually saves. The calculator lets you enter the rate from your own bill instead.

Payback assumes every kWh is used at home, a 3% annual rise in the electricity price and 0.5% a year of panel degradation, with no discounting. With the $1,200 mid-range kit an east- or west-facing balcony pays back in 8.0 years and a north-facing one in 17.3 years. A household using Rhode Island’s average of 567 kWh a month would cover about 10% of its electricity with a south-facing panel.

4Emissions

Providence is on EPA’s NPCC New England grid subregion (NEWE), which emitted 0.539 lb of CO₂ per kWh generated in 2023 (eGRID2023). A south-facing panel producing 670 kWh a year would avoid about 361 lb of CO₂.

5Common questions

Is balcony solar legal in Rhode Island?

A plug-in solar bill in Rhode Island (H 7269 (Sub A) / S 2359) did not become law. The utility’s interconnection rules apply, which usually means approval is required. H 7269 (1,200 W, exempt from interconnection, excluded from net metering) passed the House 70-0 on Jun 9, 2026 but never got a Senate vote; the 2026 session ended Jun 11-12.

How much would a balcony solar panel produce in Rhode Island?

About 670 kWh a year for an unshaded 800 W system on a south-facing railing in Providence, 473 kWh facing east or west, and 194 kWh facing north, in balco.nyc’s PVWatts model. Shade from buildings or trees opposite lowers it; the calculator models that for your address.

How much would balcony solar save in Rhode Island?

About $194 a year for a south-facing panel at Rhode Island’s average residential price of 29.0¢/kWh, with a payback of about 5.8 years on a $1,200 kit. Savings assume all the power is used at home.

Does Rhode Island pay for surplus balcony solar power?

Rhode Island has no plug-in solar law, so there is no rule for it; connecting a device that exports power normally needs the utility’s approval first. The estimates here assume surplus power earns nothing.

Do renters in Rhode Island need permission to install balcony solar?

No plug-in solar law gives a right to install one. The state’s general solar-access rules, where they exist, were mostly written for roofs; the notes above say what they cover. Otherwise a renter needs the landlord’s permission and a condo or co-op resident the board’s.

Can I use a plug-in solar panel on Rhode Island Energy's grid?

Not without its approval under current rules. Rhode Island Energy says interconnecting customers are not permitted to operate any generation before receiving an official Authorization to Interconnect, and are liable for damage from unauthorized operation. H 7269, which would have exempted devices of up to 1,200 W, passed the House in 2026 but died without a Senate vote.
