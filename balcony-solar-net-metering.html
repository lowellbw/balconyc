Exports and net metering

Do you get paid for balcony solar power you don’t use?

Almost certainly not. None of the nine state plug-in solar laws signed so far requires a utility to pay for power a balcony system sends back to the grid, five of them expressly shut these devices out of net metering, and New York’s SUNNY Act says exports “shall be uncompensated.” So the number that matters is how much you use yourself: balco.nyc’s hourly PVWatts run for an 800 W south-facing railing in New York City finds that an apartment drawing a steady 150 W uses about 60% of the output and gives the other 240 kWh a year away.

1Surplus power is given away, not sold

A plug-in panel’s power goes first to whatever the apartment is drawing at that moment: the fridge, the router, a laptop charger. Only when the panel produces more than the apartment uses does anything flow back out through the meter to the neighbors. That outflow is the surplus.

The arrangement that pays for exports is net metering, under which the kilowatt-hours you send out are credited against the ones you buy. Net metering normally comes with an interconnection application and utility approval — exactly the paperwork plug-in laws exempt these devices from. The trade the states have made is consistent: no application, no fee, no credit. This is not legal advice, and it varies by state and utility, but the safe planning assumption is that surplus earns nothing.

That makes one number decisive: the share of the panel’s output you use at the moment it is produced. balco.nyc’s calculator, like most, assumes that share is 100% and values every kWh at Con Edison’s all-in marginal rate of 34¢. That is a ceiling. To see how far below it a real apartment sits, balco.nyc ran NREL’s PVWatts V8 hour by hour for an 800 W system on a vertical, south-facing railing in New York City, with the calculator’s own parameters. The hourly run reproduces the 635.7 kWh a year in the site’s state data exactly; less the 5% railing loss the site applies to vertical panels (a factor of 0.95), that is about 604 kWh. Set hour by hour against an apartment drawing a steady 150 W, 364 kWh of it is used at home and 240 kWh leaves through the meter. At 34¢ that is a saving of about $124 a year, not the $205 the 100% assumption gives.

2What each plug-in law says about exported power

Nine states have signed plug-in solar laws, and bills in New York and California have passed their legislatures. balco.nyc read each in the legislature’s own text. Five expressly shut plug-in devices out of net metering, three say nothing about paying for exports, and Connecticut requires a warning that exports may be billed. None requires a utility to pay for them.

Silence is not a credit: where a law says nothing, balco.nyc assumes no payment unless a utility publishes a tariff for plug-in devices. New York’s bill leaves one door open: an owner may voluntarily enter an interconnection or net-metering agreement, and exports stay unpaid unless they do. Getting paid, in other words, means doing the paperwork the bill otherwise waives. 3Does the meter spin backwards?

“Spinning backwards” is the old picture of net metering: while you export, the meter subtracts, and you are billed for the difference. Whether anything like that happens with a plug-in panel depends on the meter, not the law. The Maryland Public Service Commission’s balcony-solar guidance describes what a meter that cannot track exports may do: treat the surplus as zero consumption, or add it to your consumption in error, depending on the meter type. That gives three possibilities.

  • Not counted. The surplus vanishes from the bill in both directions. This is the outcome balco.nyc’s figures assume: you lose its value and nothing more.
  • Counted as usage. A meter that registers power flowing either way as consumption bills you at the full retail rate for electricity you gave away. Connecticut’s law requires every device to carry a warning saying exactly this. In the New York example above, the 240 kWh exported would be billed at about $82 a year, cutting a $124 saving to about $42.
  • Netted. A meter set up for net metering does subtract exports. That is the only version of “spinning backwards” that pays you, and under these laws it comes with a net-metering arrangement, not with the plug.

Utilities are beginning to say which applies. Potomac Edison, a FirstEnergy utility in Maryland, states that its standard service meter has no separate register to track energy a customer feeds back to its system. Vermont’s law allows plug-in devices only on smart-metered service. In New York City, Con Edison describes a net meter for customers enrolled in net metering; what an ordinary meter records when power flows out is worth asking Con Edison before the SUNNY Act, which awaits the Governor's signature, takes effect. The practical step is the same everywhere: ask your utility, in writing, how your meter records power flowing out of the apartment.

4How much of the output an apartment actually uses

The share you use depends on how much the panel produces in each hour and how much the apartment draws in that hour. The second has a hard lower limit. Every home has a floor it never drops below: the fridge, the router, standby electronics, smoke alarms. NRDC’s 2015 study of smart-meter data from 70,000 northern California homes found that floor, the lowest hourly level, averaged 218 W; the one multifamily home in its ten-home audit, occupied by a couple it described as unusually energy-conscious, idled at 92 W. A smaller apartment will usually sit below the study average, so 100 to 300 W brackets most floors.

balco.nyc set the hourly New York output against a constant load at each of those levels. A constant floor is what an apartment draws with nobody home, so these shares hold even for an apartment empty all day; daytime cooking, working or laundry raises them.

Share of annual output used at home: New York City, vertical south-facing railing, PVWatts V8 hourly, 0.95 railing factor, no shading. For an 800 W railing, most apartments will use between about 60% and 90% of the output; a small, efficient apartment that is empty in the daytime can use less than half. Hourly averages flatter these figures slightly, since passing cloud and a cycling fridge make supply and demand miss each other within the hour; real loads sitting above the floor for much of the day push the other way.

The surplus is seasonal, and not in the direction most people expect. A vertical south-facing panel meets the low winter sun almost head-on, so in New York it produces most in winter: 69 kWh in January against 30 kWh in June, with midday peaks near 690 W. Against a 150 W floor only 43% of January’s output is used at home; in June, 96%. The kilowatt-hours given away are winter ones, which in New York are the cheaper ones, since Con Edison’s delivery charges are higher from June through September (see what a kWh costs).

Why a 400 W system uses more of its output than a 1,200 W one. The apartment’s load does not grow when you add panels. Every extra panel produces in the same midday hours as the first, when the floor is already covered, so most of its output is surplus. At a 150 W floor, going from 400 to 800 W adds 302 kWh a year of output but only 102 kWh used at home; going from 800 to 1,200 W adds another 302 kWh of output and only 49 kWh used, about $17 a year at 34¢. The table has a simple symmetry that shows why: a 400 W system against a 150 W floor behaves exactly like an 800 W system against 300 W. What matters is the ratio of system size to what the apartment draws.

5What self-consumption does to payback

Because surplus earns nothing, savings scale directly with the share you use, and payback stretches with it. At 60% self-consumption, payback is about 1.6 times the 100% figure in all three. In New York, 60% is what the hourly run gives an empty apartment with a 150 W floor, so the 5.5-year headline becomes 8.8. In Illinois it becomes 14.7 years, well over half the 25-year span the calculator models. The self-consumption shares are computed for New York’s sun; Chicago and Los Angeles will differ somewhat at the same floor, but the direction is the same. 6Batteries, timers and system size

A battery stores midday surplus for the evening, so its value is capped by how much surplus there is. balco.nyc ran the same hourly New York data with a 2 kWh battery (1.92 kWh usable, 90% round-trip efficiency) that captures each day’s surplus up to its capacity and releases all of it in the evening — a generous setup that ignores the battery’s own standby draw. The last column applies the calculator’s 3% annual price rise.

The more you already use, the less a battery has to work with; at balcony scale the ceiling is tens of dollars a year. Modeling in Germany points the same way: the nonprofit balkon.solar, using HTW Berlin’s plug-in solar simulator with a vertical west-facing array and no pay for exports, found a 2 kWh battery stretched payback from 5–6 years to about 13 for small households with small arrays, improving to about 8 only with larger arrays and higher consumption.

The cheaper ways to use more of the output cost nothing. Put the dishwasher, the washing machine or a laptop charger on a timer for the middle of the day, especially from October to March, when a south-facing railing has the most to give. And weigh size by the marginal panel rather than the average one: in the empty-apartment case above, each step up in size buys less than the one before it.

7Common questions

Do you get paid for balcony solar power you don't use?

Not under any US plug-in solar law signed as of September 2026. Utah, Maine, Maryland, Vermont and Virginia expressly exclude plug-in devices from net metering, Colorado, New Hampshire and New Jersey say nothing about payment, and Connecticut requires a warning that exports may be billed as usage. New York's SUNNY Act, which has passed both chambers and awaits the Governor's signature, says exports are uncompensated unless the owner voluntarily signs a net-metering or other compensation agreement.

Does my electric meter spin backwards with a plug-in solar panel?

Only if it is set up for net metering, which plug-in solar laws do not provide by default. On a meter that cannot track exports, the Maryland Public Service Commission says the surplus may be treated as zero consumption or erroneously added to consumption, depending on the meter type. Ask your utility how your meter records power flowing out before plugging a panel in.

Can a balcony solar panel make my electric bill go up?

It can on a meter that counts exported power as consumption, which is why Connecticut's plug-in law requires every device to warn that excess generation will be charged as usage unless the customer has a net-metering meter. In balco.nyc's New York example, an 800 W railing in an apartment drawing a steady 150 W exports about 240 kWh a year; billed at Con Edison's 34-cent rate, that would cut a $124 annual saving to about $42.

How much of a balcony solar system's output does an apartment use?

It depends on what the apartment draws while the sun is up. balco.nyc's hourly PVWatts model of an 800 W south-facing railing in New York City finds that an apartment drawing a steady 150 W uses about 60% of the output, one drawing 100 W about 46%, and one drawing 300 W about 87%. Those shares assume nobody is home in the daytime; daytime activity raises them.

Why does a smaller balcony solar system use more of its output?

Because the apartment's load stays the same while each added panel produces in the same midday hours as the first. In balco.nyc's New York model with a 150 W floor, a 400 W system uses 87% of its output, an 800 W system 60% and a 1,200 W system 46%. The step from 800 to 1,200 W adds about 302 kWh a year of output but only 49 kWh used at home.

Is a battery worth adding to balcony solar?

Rarely at balcony scale. In balco.nyc's hourly New York model, a 2 kWh battery on an 800 W railing is worth about $24 to $89 a year at Con Edison's 34-cent rate, depending on how much the apartment already uses, so it would have to cost roughly $280 to $1,020 to pay for itself in 10 years. The more electricity the household already uses in daylight, the less surplus the battery has to store.
