Con Edison

Con Edison and plug-in solar: what applies today, and after the SUNNY Act

Today Con Edison treats a plug-in panel like any other generator: its tariff allows no second source of power in a home outside its net-metering and standby rules, and New York’s Standardized Interconnection Requirements set the process, which for 50 kW or less means an online application and a certified inverter but no fee. If the SUNNY Act, which has passed both chambers and awaits the Governor's signature, becomes law, a compliant device of up to 1,200 W needs only a notice to Con Edison within 30 days of installation. balco.nyc’s month-by-month model puts what an unshaded 800 W south-facing railing saves an SC-1 customer at about $205 a year if every kWh is used ($209 with the summer delivery tier), or $124 in an apartment drawing a steady 150 W, which gives 240 kWh a year to the grid uncredited.

1Today: a generator, whatever its size

Con Edison’s electric tariff, PSC No. 10, starts from a blunt rule. General Rule 8.1, “Segregated Service”, says no other source of electric energy may be introduced in connection with a customer’s equipment except under a short list of provisions: Rider R (net metering and the Value Stack), emergency generators wired so they cannot run in parallel with the grid, a few demand-response cases, and Standby Service. Every interconnection of generating equipment must comply with Rider R or the Standby Service rules. There is no exception for small equipment.

The interconnection itself is governed by the New York State Standardized Interconnection Requirements (SIR), the Public Service Commission’s rulebook for generators and batteries of 5 MW or less that run in parallel with a utility’s distribution system. The current edition took effect on 9 February 2026. It has no minimum size. The only systems it leaves out are those “neither designed to operate, nor operating, in parallel” with the grid, and a plug-in microinverter is designed to do exactly that: it synchronizes with the voltage at the outlet and pushes current into it. Con Edison’s own solar guide puts it from the utility’s side: solar of 5 MW or less is subject to the SIR.

So under the rules in force on 23 September 2026, a balcony panel feeding a Con Edison customer’s outlet without an approved interconnection is operating outside the tariff. This page is not legal advice. The useful questions are what the lawful route involves and what New York’s pending bill would change.

2What the SIR asks of a system under 50 kW

The SIR splits projects at 50 kW AC. Below that line is the expedited process nearly all home solar uses; Con Edison’s 2022 solar guide put about 98% of its solar jobs in it. An 800 W railing would take the same path as a rooftop array.

Two things make the route harder, or dearer, in New York City than the table suggests.

The network. SIR Exception 3 says systems of 50 kW or less in underground secondary network areas may need more information and review, and that “in some cases, interconnection may not be allowed or approved.” Con Edison’s guide says network grids are used extensively throughout Manhattan, Brooklyn, Queens and the Bronx. For many apartment dwellers the small-system path comes with an extra review.

The monthly charge. Net-metered residential solar interconnected since 1 January 2022 pays a Customer Benefit Contribution on panel capacity. Con Edison’s 2026 statement sets it at $1.29 per kW DC a month for SC-1 solar on Phase One net metering: about $12 a year for 800 W of panels.

None of this was written with a plug-in device in mind. balco.nyc found nothing in Con Edison’s distributed-generation pages or solar guide that addresses plug-in or balcony systems. Whether an outlet connection belongs on a single-line drawing at all is a wiring question, covered on the safety page.

3What the SUNNY Act would change

The SUNNY Act (S8512C / A9111C) adds a subdivision on “portable solar generation devices” to section 66-j of the Public Service Law. balco.nyc read the text that passed, on the Assembly’s bill page. It covers a device of up to 1,200 W of continuous AC inverter output that plugs into a standard outlet, is listed by an accredited laboratory “as a complete plug-in photovoltaic system”, and stops energizing the building during an outage. The bill has passed both chambers and awaits the Governor's signature.

Two clocks follow a signature. The Act takes effect on the 90th day after it becomes law, and every electric utility then has 30 days to post a “simple notification form” on its website, so Con Edison’s form would be due about 120 days after signing. An owner would then have 30 days from installing to file it. The bill has not yet been delivered to the Governor, so neither clock has started; the SUNNY Act page tracks its status.

The Act keeps the SIR route open for anyone who wants credit for exports. For an 800 W railing the prize is small. The 240 kWh a year that balco.nyc’s model has an empty 150 W apartment exporting would be worth at most about $82 at 34¢; less the $12 contribution, roughly $70 a year, before the paperwork and any network review. Nor does the Act give anyone a right to hang a panel. Permission is a separate question for co-op and condo boards, landlords and NYCHA, landmarked facades and fire escapes.

4What a Con Edison smart meter does with exported power

Con Edison began installing smart meters in 2017, more than 4.8 million gas and electric meters in what it called the largest capital project in its history, and it shows customers their electric use in 15-minute increments. On paper the meters can also see power leaving an apartment. Con Edison’s 2015 smart-meter business plan, filed with the Public Service Commission, lists the Commission’s minimum requirement that “AMI systems must provide net metering”, and its own specification called for meters that support net metering, remote configuration and downloadable firmware. The plan also counted as a saving that smart meters would end the need for a separate meter at solar sites.

What is not published is how an ordinary residential meter, on an account not enrolled in net metering, is configured to treat power flowing out. It could ignore it, net it against use, or add it to use. The tariff says bills are based on “the registration of the Company-owned meters”, so the configuration decides the bill. balco.nyc found no Con Edison or Commission document that says which applies to an SC-1 account without net metering. Ask Con Edison in writing how the meter on your account registers power flowing out, and how it would be billed, before plugging anything in.

Other states show why the answer matters. The Maryland Public Service Commission warns that a meter unable to track exports may treat them as zero or add them to consumption in error, depending on the meter, and Connecticut’s law requires every device to warn that surplus generation will be “charged to the customer as usage” without a net-metering meter. New York’s bill is worded differently: it bars “any fee or charge” for feeding energy back into the grid, which on its face would rule out billing exports as usage. How the Commission and Con Edison would implement that is not yet known. If exports were billed as usage, the 150 W example in the next section would fall from about $124 to about $42 a year. The three possible meter outcomes are set out on the surplus-power page.

5Month by month: what an 800 W railing saves on SC-1

Con Edison’s standard residential rate, SC-1 Rate I, has one seasonal feature. From 1 February 2026 (Leaf 388, Case 25-E-0072) the energy delivery charge is 16.402¢/kWh in every month, except that from June through September each kWh above the first 250 in the month costs 18.858¢. The customer charge of $21.00 a month does not change with use, and a panel cannot reduce it. Supply and the other per-kWh adjustments are set separately, most of them monthly, and this table does not try to forecast them.

A panel’s kWh come off the top of the bill. In a summer month where the apartment still uses more than 250 kWh after the panel, every solar kWh avoids the upper tier: 2.456¢ more, about 2.6¢ with the 4.5% city sales tax. balco.nyc added that to the flat 34¢ marginal rate for June to September. That slightly overstates the summer figure, since the annual 34¢ already averages in some upper-tier kWh; below 250 kWh a month there is no summer premium at all. Output is balco.nyc’s PVWatts V8 run for New York City, 800 W on an unshaded vertical south-facing railing, times the 0.95 railing factor. The split at a steady 150 W draw comes from the same run, hour by hour, as on the surplus-power page.

  • This is a best case. Shade from the building opposite, or a railing facing east or west, cuts every column; output by direction has the other orientations, and the calculator models your own block.

6Common questions

Does Con Edison allow plug-in solar today?

Only through the same process as any other generator. Con Edison's tariff (General Rule 8.1) bars any other source of electricity in a customer's installation except under its net-metering, standby or emergency-generator provisions, and interconnection follows New York's Standardized Interconnection Requirements. For systems of 50 kW or less that means an online application, a UL 1741 SB-certified inverter, a signed contract and a verification test, with no application fee. Con Edison publishes no separate route for plug-in devices.

Will I have to tell Con Edison about a balcony solar panel under the SUNNY Act?

Yes, but only tell, not ask. If the SUNNY Act, which has passed both chambers and awaits the Governor's signature, becomes law, the owner must notify the utility within 30 days of installing a device, using a simple web form each utility must post within 30 days of the law taking effect. The form may ask only for name and contact details, service address and account number, the device's make and model, and its rated size. Con Edison could not require approval, fees or extra equipment.

Is there a fee to connect small solar to Con Edison?

There is no application fee for systems of 50 kW AC or less under New York's Standardized Interconnection Requirements; Con Edison charges $750 above that. Net-metered residential solar interconnected since 1 January 2022 does pay a monthly Customer Benefit Contribution, $1.29 per kW DC a month on SC-1 in 2026, about $12 a year for 800 W of panels. The SUNNY Act would bar any fee or charge related to a compliant plug-in device.

Does a Con Edison smart meter record power a balcony panel sends to the grid?

Con Edison's smart meters were specified to support net metering, which implies they can measure power in both directions, and Con Edison shows electric use in 15-minute increments. What Con Edison has not published is how an ordinary, non-net-metered residential meter is configured to treat exports: ignored, netted, or counted as usage. Ask Con Edison in writing before plugging a panel in.

How much does an 800 W balcony panel save a Con Edison customer?

In balco.nyc's model, an unshaded, vertical south-facing 800 W railing in New York City produces about 604 kWh a year, worth about $205 at Con Edison's 34-cent marginal rate if every kWh is used at home, or $209 counting the June-to-September delivery tier. In an apartment drawing a steady 150 W with nobody home, about 364 kWh is used and the saving falls to about $127, or $124 at the flat rate; the other 240 kWh go to the grid uncredited.

Does Con Edison charge more for electricity in summer?

On standard residential SC-1 service, yes for kWh above the first 250 in a month. From 1 February 2026 the energy delivery charge is 16.402 cents per kWh all year, but from June through September each kWh above 250 in the month costs 18.858 cents. The supply charge also changes monthly. For a south-facing balcony panel the summer tier is worth only about $4 a year, because a vertical south panel produces most in winter.
