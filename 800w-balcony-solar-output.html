How much does an 800 W balcony solar system produce? By state and direction

An 800 W balcony system hung vertically on an unshaded, south-facing railing makes about 660 kWh a year in the median state’s largest city, from 510 kWh in Honolulu to 819 kWh in Albuquerque. balco.nyc ran NREL’s PVWatts for the largest city in every state, and DC, with the calculator’s own parameters: facing east or west costs about a quarter of that, facing north about 70%, and tilting the panel to 35° adds about 60%. Every figure is a ceiling for a balcony with an open view — the building across the street can take a large bite out of it.

1The short answer, and where it comes from

There is no single answer to how much an 800 W balcony system produces — only an answer per place, per direction and per mounting angle. So balco.nyc computed them. For the largest city in each of the 50 states and Washington, DC, by the Census Bureau’s 2025 population estimates, we asked NREL’s PVWatts V8 eleven questions: what an 800 W system makes hung vertically on a railing facing each of the eight compass points, and what it makes facing south on a bracket at 35°, 60° and 70°. That is 561 simulations, each an hour-by-hour year of typical weather from the National Solar Radiation Database. (The Department of Energy renamed NREL the National Laboratory of the Rockies in December 2025; PVWatts now lives at nlr.gov.)

The parameters are the calculator’s own, not PVWatts’s defaults: a premium module on an open rack, 14% system losses plus a monthly urban soiling profile of 3–7%, a microinverter at 96.5% efficiency, and ground reflectance of 0.20. PVWatts assumes an unobstructed panel, so every result below is then multiplied by the site’s railing factor, exactly as the calculator does: 0.95 for a vertical panel, whose lower edge the railing and mounting hardware partly clip, 0.97 at 70°, 0.98 at 60° and 0.99 at 35°. All of it is documented on the methodology page.

2A rule of thumb: kWh per watt

Divide the south-facing vertical result by the panel rating and a usable rule falls out. A vertical, unshaded, south-facing railing makes roughly 0.75 to 0.95 kWh per watt per year across most of the US. In balco.nyc’s runs, 39 of the 51 cities land in that band and the median is 0.82; widen it to 0.7–1.0 and it covers 43. For an 800 W system that is about 600 to 760 kWh a year. The watts are the panel’s nameplate rating, the number on the box.

The cities outside the band are mostly the ones you would guess. Above it sits the high, dry interior West: Albuquerque, Las Vegas, Cheyenne and Denver all clear 1.0, with Phoenix and Sioux Falls just behind. Below it are Seattle, Portland (Oregon) and Anchorage, whose winters give a railing little to work with; Charleston, West Virginia, and Houston at 0.72; and Honolulu, which finishes last for a reason section 3 explains.

3East, west and north: what the direction costs

Missing south by 45° costs little. A railing facing south-east or south-west keeps about 95% of the south figure in the median city (93% for south-east, 96% for south-west). East and west are the real step down, to about 74%, and the two are close to mirror images. The sun’s path is symmetric about noon, so any gap between them comes from each city’s typical weather: when in the day cloud tends to arrive, and the afternoon heat that slightly lowers a panel’s efficiency. West beats east in 13 of the 51 cities, most clearly Anchorage (by 17%), Los Angeles (13%), Portland, Oregon (11%) and Seattle (8%); east beats west by about 10% in Denver and Albuquerque.

North is where rules of thumb exaggerate. A north-facing vertical panel makes about 29% of the south figure in the median city, roughly 200 kWh a year from 800 W. It is not zero, for two reasons. A vertical panel sees half the sky dome whichever way it faces, and the diffuse light scattered by the atmosphere and by cloud arrives from all of it. And in summer the sun rises and sets north of the east–west line, so a north wall gets some direct light early and late. The clearer the sky, the further north falls behind: it makes 25% of the south figure in Albuquerque and Las Vegas, where direct sun is most of the resource, and 36% in Houston. Across all 51 cities a south railing makes a median 3.4 times what a north one does — a bigger gap than the 1.6 times between the best and worst city.

Honolulu is the exception that shows the geometry at work. At 21°N it lies south of the Tropic of Cancer, so for part of the summer the noon sun is in the northern half of the sky, and for most of the year it climbs high enough to strike a wall at a glancing angle. It is the only city where east (107% of south) and west (105%) beat south, where north reaches 42%, and where south-west is the best vertical direction. A south railing there ranks last of the 51 — while the same panel tilted to 35° ranks ninth.

4Why a railing makes less than a roof

Tilt the south-facing panel from vertical to 35°, roughly a rooftop angle, and the median city gains 59%, from 658 to 1,053 kWh. A 60° bracket gains 42% and a 70° bracket 29%. The gain grows toward the equator: 33% in Anchorage, 42–48% across most of the northern tier from Montana to Maine, 81–85% in Jacksonville, New Orleans and Houston, and 129% in Honolulu.

The reason is geometry, and one worked example shows it. At New York’s latitude, 40.7°N, the noon sun stands about 26° above the horizon at the December solstice and about 73° at the June solstice (90° minus the latitude, plus the sun’s declination of ±23.4°). A vertical panel faces the horizon, so in December the noon sun strikes it 26° off square and delivers about 90% of its full strength — the cosine of 26°. In June it strikes 73° off square and delivers about 30%. A panel at 35° faces 55° up the sky, and gets about 87% in December and 95% in June. At noon on the winter solstice a railing and a roof are nearly level; at the summer solstice the roof wins by a factor of three.

The sky matters too. A vertical panel sees exactly half the sky dome, against about 91% for a panel at 35°, so it collects half the diffuse light a flat surface would. It also sees half the ground, and at the runs’ 0.20 reflectance that adds light equal to a tenth of what falls on the ground — a real contribution for a railing, under 2% for a panel at 35°. None of this makes a vertical panel a mistake. A railing is often the only surface a renter can use, and whether a bracket is allowed at all is a question for the building, not the model.

5Why a south railing is a winter panel

The same geometry flips the seasons. On a vertical south railing, 48 of the 51 cities make more electricity from December to February than from June to August — a median of 1.84 times as much — and June is the weakest month of the year in 48 of them. Tilt the same panel to 35° and the pattern reverses: only Honolulu makes more in winter, and December is the weakest month in 41 cities.

New York shows the shape. An unshaded vertical south panel there makes 69 kWh in January and 30 in June. At 35° the same panel makes 97 in July and 57 in December.

The three exceptions are informative. In Anchorage, at 61°N, the December noon sun clears the horizon by barely 5°, so a railing makes about a third as much in winter as in summer. In Seattle and Portland, Oregon, the winter weather does the damage: a vertical south panel makes 101–108 kWh from December to February there, against 226 in Minneapolis and 187 in Billings at about the same latitude.

Two winter effects sit partly outside these runs. They fix ground reflectance at 0.20 all year and allow 1% of losses for snow, so they neither credit the extra light a snow-covered street or roof bounces up nor charge for a heavy fall that sits on the panel. For snowy cities, treat the winter months as approximate in both directions.

7Ceilings, not expectations: shade, and New York

Every figure on this page assumes the panel sees an open sky. Real balconies rarely do. The building across the street, the balcony slab above, a street tree, a neighbor’s air conditioner: none of them are in these runs, and in a dense city they can remove a large share of the year — most of all in winter, when the low sun that suits a railing is also the sun most easily blocked. Does my balcony get enough sun? explains why obstruction and floor height can matter more than direction, and how to calculate your balcony’s output walks through applying them. The calculator takes any US address and a description of what stands opposite, and runs the same model with the shade included. A single year will also differ from these typical-year figures; PVWatts shows an expected range around each result, based on 30 years of weather.

New York City is the useful local check. For a point at City Hall, the unshaded runs put a vertical railing at 604 kWh a year facing south, 562–567 facing south-west or south-east, 421–446 facing west or east and 167 facing north; a 60° bracket reaches 872 and 35° reaches 988. For a New York address the calculator also builds a 3D model of the surrounding buildings, which takes those ceilings down to what a particular floor and wall actually see.

New York ranks 43rd of 51 for a south railing, which is why the city’s case rests on the price of a kWh rather than on its sun. Whether a few hundred kWh a year justifies a kit where you live is the subject of whether balcony solar is worth it.

8Common questions

How many kWh does an 800 W balcony solar system produce per year?

Hung vertically on an unshaded south-facing railing, about 660 kWh a year in the median US state's largest city, according to balco.nyc's PVWatts runs for all 51: from 510 kWh in Honolulu to 819 kWh in Albuquerque. Facing east or west it makes about 74% of that, facing north about 29%, and on a 35-degree bracket about 159%. Shade from nearby buildings reduces all of these figures.

How much does an east-facing balcony solar panel produce?

An 800 W panel hung vertically on an unshaded east-facing railing makes about 74% of what a south-facing one would, roughly 490 kWh a year in the median US state's largest city; the middle 80% of cities fall between 70% and 82%. West-facing is almost the same. In balco.nyc's PVWatts runs, west beat east in 13 of the 51 cities and east beat west in the rest, usually by a few percent.

How much does a north-facing balcony solar panel produce?

About 29% of a south-facing panel in the median US city, or roughly 200 kWh a year for an 800 W system on an unshaded vertical railing; balco.nyc's runs range from 149 kWh in Seattle to 241 kWh in Sioux Falls. It is not zero because a vertical panel sees half the sky whichever way it faces, and in summer the sun rises and sets north of due east and due west.

How much more does a tilted balcony panel produce than a vertical one?

Across balco.nyc's PVWatts runs for the largest city in every state, tilting a south-facing 800 W panel from vertical to 35 degrees raises annual output by a median of 59%; a 60-degree bracket adds 42% and 70 degrees 29%. The gain is smallest far north (33% in Anchorage) and largest near the tropics (85% in Houston, 129% in Honolulu), because a high summer sun strikes a vertical panel at a glancing angle.

Does a balcony solar panel produce more in winter or summer?

On a vertical south-facing railing, usually winter. In 48 of the 51 US cities balco.nyc modeled, an unshaded vertical south panel makes more from December to February than from June to August, a median of 1.84 times as much, because the low winter sun meets a vertical panel nearly head-on. The exceptions are Anchorage, Seattle and Portland, Oregon. A panel tilted to 35 degrees does the reverse and produces most in summer.

Which state gets the most from a balcony solar panel?

For an unshaded vertical south-facing railing, New Mexico, Nevada, Wyoming and Colorado lead: Albuquerque, Las Vegas, Cheyenne and Denver each make just over 800 kWh a year from 800 W in balco.nyc's PVWatts runs. Seattle, Portland (Oregon), Anchorage and Honolulu come last, at 510 to 535 kWh. Output is only part of the economics: what a kWh costs locally decides what that output is worth.

How much does an 800 W balcony system produce in New York City?

Unshaded, a vertical south-facing railing at New York's City Hall makes about 604 kWh a year in balco.nyc's PVWatts run; east or west 421 to 446, north 167, and a south panel on a 35-degree bracket 988. Buildings opposite reduce those figures; for a specific New York address the calculator models them in 3D.
