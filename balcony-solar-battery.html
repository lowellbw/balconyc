Batteries and storage

Does a battery make balcony solar pay?

Rarely, at New York prices. balco.nyc’s hourly PVWatts model of an 800 W south-facing railing in New York City finds that a 2 kWh battery lets an apartment use 132 to 234 more kWh a year, worth $45 to $80 at Con Edison’s 34¢, so it pays for itself in 10 years only if it costs under about $520 to $910. The comparable plug-in batteries Stiftung Warentest tested in 2026 sold for about €700 to €1,000, roughly $800 to $1,140. And in New York City, a lithium-ion battery over 1 kWh kept indoors appears to fall under energy-storage rules that call for listing, FDNY approval and permits.

1What a battery adds to an 800 W railing, hour by hour

A plug-in battery earns money one way: it stores midday power the apartment cannot use and releases it later, instead of electricity bought from the utility. Because no US plug-in solar law signed so far pays for exports (see what happens to surplus power), each stored kWh is worth the full retail price, and the surplus caps what a battery can do.

To measure that, balco.nyc ran NREL’s PVWatts V8 hour by hour for an 800 W vertical, south-facing railing in New York City and in Los Angeles, with the calculator’s own parameters. The two runs reproduce the site’s state data exactly, 635.7 and 758.3 kWh a year; after the 0.95 railing factor the site applies to vertical panels, that is 604 kWh in New York and 720 kWh in Los Angeles. For each of the 8,760 hours, the panel first supplied a steady apartment load of 100, 150 or 200 W. Any surplus charged a battery with 1, 2 or 5 kWh of usable capacity, which then fed the load whenever the panel fell short, through the evening and night, without ever exporting. Round-trip efficiency is 78%, a laboratory figure from HTW Berlin explained in section 5.

balco.nyc’s surplus-power guide gave a 2 kWh battery $24 to $89 a year, assuming 90% efficiency and including a 300 W apartment. That estimate used 1.92 kWh of usable capacity and emptied the battery every evening; this model carries charge from day to day, and rerun on the same assumptions it agrees to within 4 kWh a year. The figures above are 10% to 13% lower only because they use the measured 78%. At 90% the 2 kWh column would read $88, $70 and $52.

2Why a bigger battery adds almost nothing

The 5 kWh column barely differs from the 2 kWh one: $63 against $62 a year at 150 W. The surplus, not the battery, is the constraint. In balco.nyc’s New York run with a 150 W floor, the railing’s surplus averages 0.66 kWh a day. It is under 0.5 kWh on 215 days of the year and over 2 kWh on only 35.

The surplus is also seasonal. A vertical south-facing panel meets the low winter sun almost head-on, so in New York it produces most from October to March, and that is when there is anything to store: 79% of the 2 kWh battery’s annual output comes in those six months, and in June and July it has almost nothing to do. Los Angeles is similar, at 84%.

So the first kilowatt-hour of capacity does most of the work. At 150 W, 1 kWh is worth $47 a year, a second kWh adds $15, and the next three add $2 between them. Expressed as the price per kWh of capacity at which a battery repays itself in 10 years, that is $537 for a 1 kWh unit, $354 for 2 kWh and $145 for 5 kWh. The more the apartment already uses in daylight, the lower all three go. The same diminishing return applies to panels; see how big a system to buy.

The best neutral price data balco.nyc found are German. Stiftung Warentest, the German consumer testing foundation, bought five plug-in batteries of 1.92 to 2.69 kWh in autumn 2025 and found them selling for about €700 to €1,000 in February 2026, roughly $800 to $1,140 at the European Central Bank’s reference rate of $1.1411 on 23 September 2026. The consumer advice center Verbraucherzentrale Niedersachsen put them at €200 to €400 per kWh of capacity in July 2026, about $228 to $456. balco.nyc found no neutral survey of US prices.

At those prices a 2 kWh battery in New York repays itself in about 9 to 14 years at $800 and 12 to 19 years at $1,140. Added to a kit, it shortens the payback only in the emptiest apartment, where the panels alone waste the most; in the 150 W case it stretches 8.8 years to 9.6. A battery helps most where the panels are oversized for the household.

4Where time-of-use pricing changes the answer

A flat price undervalues a battery wherever evening electricity costs more, because evening is when it discharges: in both cities, 55% of the 150 W apartment’s battery output lands between 4 and 9 pm. A time-of-use plan prices that. Southern California Edison’s TOU-D-4-9PM plan, as SCE publishes it for customers who buy both delivery and generation from SCE, charges 58¢ from 4 to 9 pm on summer weekdays (46¢ at weekends) and 34¢ otherwise; from October to May it charges 51¢ from 4 to 9 pm, 33¢ from 8 am to 4 pm and 37¢ overnight. The city of Los Angeles itself buys from its municipal utility, LADWP, whose rates differ; SCE’s plan is used here as a published example of Southern California time-of-use pricing, applied to Los Angeles’s sun.

5Efficiency, standby power and wear

The 78% comes from HTW Berlin’s 2026 Stromspeicher-Inspektion, a laboratory comparison of 12 home battery systems. The batteries themselves lost little, with efficiencies of 87.9% to 97.1%. The loss is in converting stored power back to household current at low output: even in highly efficient systems, discharge efficiency fell below 95% under 500 W and below 90% under 300 W, and a balcony battery spends the whole evening at 100 to 200 W. Counting both conversions and the battery, HTW measured 78% for a very efficient system delivering an average 200 W, and 63% for an inefficient one. Those are larger home systems; Stiftung Warentest measured its plug-in units too, but its figures are behind a paywall and balco.nyc found no freely published laboratory figure for plug-in batteries. Treat 78% as an estimate and 90% as an optimistic case.

Standby draw can matter as much as efficiency. Electronics, Wi-Fi and power sensors run around the clock; HTW measured 4 to 64 W across its systems with the battery empty. In balco.nyc’s New York model, even 4 W, the best HTW found, cuts the 2 kWh battery’s value by $10 to $11 a year; 10 W cuts the 150 W apartment’s $62 to $34 and the 200 W apartment’s $45 to about $17. A battery’s own consumption can cancel most of what it saves.

Wear is less of a problem than it sounds. The model’s 2 kWh battery in New York runs 75 to 132 equivalent full cycles a year, under 2,000 in 15 years. Most plug-in batteries use lithium iron phosphate (LFP) cells, according to Verbraucherzentrale Niedersachsen, which reports that manufacturers typically claim over 6,000 cycles and give 10-year warranties. In Sandia National Laboratories’ tests of commercial cells, LFP ran 2,500 to 9,000 equivalent full cycles, and most had not yet fallen to 80% of their capacity by the end of the reported tests. Losing capacity costs less than it seems, too: at 80% capacity the model’s New York battery is worth only 3% to 6% less, because it is rarely full. Heat, age and the electronics are likelier limits. Sandia found LFP cells lose capacity faster as temperature rises from 15 to 35°C, and a battery on a sunny balcony runs warm. A 15-year payback also outlasts a 10-year warranty.

6Permission and fire rules for batteries in New York City

New York City regulates batteries far more tightly than panels. Since 26 October 2025, Department of Buildings rules have adopted a city-modified version of NFPA 855, the national standard for stationary energy storage. Its threshold for lithium-ion storage indoors is 1 kWh, set at FDNY’s request to reflect the hazards of smaller systems, and its scope expressly includes “mobile and portable ESS installed in a stationary situation.” Unless the standard exempts it, indoor storage above the threshold must be listed to UL 9540 and backed by UL 9540A fire testing, hold an FDNY Certificate of Approval, and go through DOB filing, permits and registration. FDNY’s own guide requires a Certificate of Approval for every stationary battery system except outdoor units of 2 kWh or less, a DOB work permit for a stationary system of any size, and an FDNY inspection and permit for every indoor one. In one- and two-family houses, storage may not go in a home’s living area or in sleeping units.

What is unclear is whether any of this reaches a consumer battery plugged into an outlet. None of these documents mentions plug-in balcony batteries, and balco.nyc found no FDNY or DOB guidance on whether one counts as an installation. Read plainly, a 2 kWh lithium-ion unit left in place inside an apartment is above the indoor threshold, and one on a balcony would still need a DOB permit as a stationary system. Until the city says otherwise, assume a battery needs approval: ask FDNY and DOB before buying, and your landlord or board as well (see renters and condos and co-ops). This is not legal advice.

State law does not settle it. New York’s SUNNY Act, which has passed both chambers and awaits the Governor's signature, covers photovoltaic systems; its text never mentions batteries or storage. Maine’s law, by contrast, expressly covers “plug-in battery systems” that connect through a standard outlet.

On fire risk, FDNY counted 268 lithium-ion battery fires in 2023 and 277 in 2024; deaths fell from 18 to 6, and to one in 2025 as of its 16 December year-end report. Its lithium-ion task force was created in response to fires caused by micromobility devices such as e-bikes, and the published totals do not separate out home storage; balco.nyc has no fire data for plug-in balcony batteries. Quality varies: in Stiftung Warentest’s test, three of the five batteries were rated deficient for electromagnetic interference, and water got into the built-in sockets of four during a heavy-rain test, although they were approved for outdoor use.

Nor is a plug-in battery a blackout plan. Whatever feeds the wall outlet must shut off when the grid does, like any plug-in inverter (see whether plug-in solar is safe); backup comes only from a separate off-grid socket, which the Warentest units had. That is closer to a portable power station, a different product compared in portable solar vs plug-in solar.

7Common questions

Is a battery worth adding to balcony solar?

Rarely in New York City. balco.nyc's hourly model of an 800 W south-facing railing finds a 2 kWh battery adds $45 to $80 a year at Con Edison's 34-cent rate, depending on how much the apartment already uses in daylight, so it pays back in 10 years only if it costs under about $520 to $910. Added to a $1,200 kit, an $800 battery shortens the payback only in the lightest-use case balco.nyc modeled, an apartment drawing 100 W; at 150 W it stretches 8.8 years to 9.6.

What size battery suits an 800 W balcony solar system?

Small. In balco.nyc's New York model, an apartment drawing a steady 150 W has an average surplus of only 0.66 kWh a day, so a 1 kWh battery is worth $47 a year, 2 kWh $62 and 5 kWh $63. Beyond about 2 kWh there is almost nothing left to store, and most of the surplus a vertical south-facing panel makes comes from October to March.

How long does a balcony solar battery take to pay for itself?

In balco.nyc's New York model, a 2 kWh battery bought for $800 pays back in about 9 to 14 years depending on the household's daytime use, and one bought for $1,140 in about 12 to 19 years. That assumes 78% round-trip efficiency, no standby draw and a 3% yearly rise in electricity prices; a battery that draws power on standby takes longer, and many carry 10-year warranties.

Does time-of-use pricing make a balcony battery pay?

It helps, because a battery discharges in the evening when time-of-use prices peak. In balco.nyc's Los Angeles model, 55% of a 2 kWh battery's output falls between 4 and 9 pm for an apartment drawing 150 W, and valued at Southern California Edison's TOU-D-4-9PM prices it is worth about $105 a year, against $77 at California's EIA average price.

Can I keep a lithium-ion battery for balcony solar in my New York City apartment?

Possibly not without approval. Since 26 October 2025, New York City's Department of Buildings rules set a 1 kWh threshold for lithium-ion storage indoors, above which a system needs UL 9540 listing, an FDNY Certificate of Approval and DOB permits, and the rules cover portable storage installed in a stationary situation. Neither FDNY nor DOB has published guidance on plug-in balcony batteries specifically, so ask both before buying. This is not legal advice.

Does a balcony solar battery provide backup power in a blackout?

Not through the wall outlet. Anything feeding power into a home's outlet must shut off when the grid goes down, so a plug-in battery can supply backup only through its own separate off-grid socket, if it has one. Plug-in solar without a battery produces nothing at all during an outage.
