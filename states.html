Balcony solar, state by state

Which states allow plug-in balcony solar, and what a panel on a railing would produce and save in each. Every figure comes from the same model as the balco.nyc calculator, and every law is sourced and dated.

Last updated: September 23, 2026

The short answer

As of 23 September 2026, 9 states have signed a plug-in solar law: Colorado, Connecticut, Maine, Maryland, New Hampshire, New Jersey, Utah, Vermont, Virginia. 5 of them are already in force. Bills awaiting a governor’s signature: California and New York. Everywhere else, a device that feeds power into a home circuit still needs the utility’s approval.

What a balcony panel is worth depends far more on the price of electricity than on the sunshine. Across the 51 states and DC, the electricity price explains 87% of the variation in payback time; how much a south-facing panel produces explains 2%. Albuquerque gets the most from a south-facing railing (819 kWh a year) and Honolulu the least (510), but payback is fastest in California, Hawaii, Massachusetts, where electricity is dearest.

How this table is made

Law: researched state by state from legislature bill pages and chaptered text where they could be reached, with the sources and review date on each state’s page. Price: U.S. EIA average residential revenue divided by sales over twelve months. Output: NREL PVWatts, run with the calculator’s own parameters for each largest city’s city hall, less a 5% railing loss. Payback: the calculator’s arithmetic, with a 3% annual price rise and 0.5% yearly degradation. Output assumes no shading from buildings opposite, so it is a ceiling; the calculator models your actual address. Slowest payback: Washington, Louisiana, Tennessee.

About

About balco.nyc Methodology llms.txt

balco.nyc is an independent, free balcony-solar calculator: it estimates what a plug-in solar panel would produce and save at any US address, with a 3D shade model for New York City buildings. Not affiliated with the City of New York, Con Edison, NYSERDA, NREL, the EIA, the EPA, or any seller of solar equipment. NYC results carry a modeled uncertainty range of about 15%. Plug-in solar rules vary by state; in New York the SUNNY Act has passed both chambers and awaits the Governor's signature.
