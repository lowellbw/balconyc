Glossary

Balcony solar glossary: the terms, with the numbers behind them

Each of the 38 terms below comes with a plain definition and the figure balco.nyc uses for it, from its calculator, its own PVWatts runs or its reading of the state laws: anti-islanding means an inverter stops within 2 seconds of a blackout, the railing factor for a vertical mount is 0.95, and nine states had signed a plug-in solar law as of 23 September 2026. One figure is new here. By balco.nyc’s arithmetic, an unshaded, south-facing 800 W railing panel in New York City making 604 kWh a year has a capacity factor of 8.6%, about a third of US utility-scale solar’s 24.4%.

1The hardware and the household circuit

The terms that matter describe what happens when a small inverter feeds power into a circuit built to carry it the other way.

Plug-in solar (balcony solar, Balkonkraftwerk) · 1,200 W cap in 8 of 9 US laws
A small grid-tied solar system, usually one to four panels on a railing or wall, whose microinverter plugs into a household outlet. Germany calls it a Balkonkraftwerk and caps it at 800 VA; eight of the nine signed US laws cap it at 1,200 W. See what balcony solar is.
Microinverter · 96.5% efficient, DC/AC ratio 1.1
A small inverter that turns one or two panels’ direct current into grid-synchronized alternating current. balco.nyc models 96.5% efficiency and 1.1 watts of panel per inverter watt, so little is lost at the inverter’s limit: a vertical south panel in New York reaches 90% of it about 5 hours a year. See hours near full output.
Anti-islanding · stop within 2 seconds
Keeping an inverter from energizing wires after the grid fails, which could shock a line worker who believes the line is dead. Under UL 1741 and IEEE 1547 it must stop within 2 seconds of losing the grid, which is also why a plug-in system gives no backup power. Maine’s law asks for 0.2 seconds. See blackouts and live plugs.
Backfeed · 25 A on a 15 A circuit, worst case
Current entering a circuit at an outlet instead of from the breaker panel, so appliances can draw from both while the breaker sees only its share. balco.nyc’s worst case: a 1,200 W inverter on a 15 A circuit lets part of the wire carry 25 A, 67% over its rating, without tripping. See the circuit arithmetic.
Branch circuit · 15 A × 120 V = 1,800 W
The wiring from one breaker to its outlets, commonly 15 A (1,800 W at 120 V) or 20 A. A 1,200 W inverter draws 10 A, two-thirds of a 15 A rating. A dedicated circuit removes the backfeed worst case; Maine requires one above 420 W. See the safety checklist.
GFCI · 28.6 ms limit; 32.8–36.7 ms under backfeed
A ground-fault circuit interrupter cuts power when current leaks to ground through water or a person; its standard, UL 943, evaluates it for one-way current only. In UL Solutions’ backfeed tests, GFCI breakers took 32.8 to 36.7 milliseconds to clear a simulated body fault, beyond the 28.6 ms limit; GFCI receptacles met it. See GFCIs and backfeed.

2Standards and certification

Three documents and one federal definition decide what “certified” means for a plug-in system, and a listing to one says nothing about the others.

UL 1741 · edition 3, 2021
The US safety standard for inverters and other grid-interconnection equipment, used with IEEE 1547; edition 3 is dated 28 September 2021. A microinverter listed to it was evaluated alone, not with panels, cord or circuit. Connecticut’s is the only signed plug-in law naming it. See the certification vocabulary.
IEEE 1547 · 2018 edition
The IEEE standard for connecting distributed generators to the grid: how they respond to voltage and frequency, and how they avoid islanding. The 2018 edition replaced the 2003 original. It governs grid behavior, not shock or fire safety; Vermont’s law names IEEE 1547-2018. See how the documents differ.
UL 3700 · outline, edition 1, 11 Dec 2025
UL’s Outline of Investigation for Interactive Plug-In PV Equipment and Systems, the first US safety document for a plug-in system as a whole: an outline, not a consensus standard. As of 23 September 2026 no complete system is listed to it; a few microinverters claim certification. Only Maine’s and Vermont’s laws require a system listing. See UL 3700 explained.
NRTL and “listed” · 29 CFR 1910.7
A nationally recognized testing laboratory, recognized by OSHA under 29 CFR 1910.7 to test, list and label products independently of their makers; UL LLC, CSA Group and Intertek are among them. “Listed” means named in such a lab’s list. Every signed plug-in law accepts labs other than UL, except Colorado’s 2027 sales rule. See how to check a listing.

3The law and permission

Most plug-in laws settle whether the utility gets a say, not whether anyone else does. These are summaries, not legal advice; details vary by state and utility.

Plug-in solar law · 9 states signed, 5 in force
A state statute letting a small plug-in system connect without utility approval, under a wattage cap and a certification rule. balco.nyc’s check of every legislature on 23 September 2026 counts nine signed laws, five in force (Utah, Maine, Colorado, Maryland and Vermont), covering 5.2% of US residents. New York’s SUNNY Act (S8512C / A9111C) has passed both chambers and awaits the Governor's signature; it grants no right to install. See every law compared.
Interconnection · waived by all 9 signed laws
The utility’s approval process for a generator running in parallel with the grid: application, agreement, sometimes fees. All nine signed laws bar the utility from requiring approval, fees or extra equipment, and eight expressly exempt the device from interconnection review. Some still require notice. See exempt versus a right to install.
Right to install · 4 of 9 laws; Vermont’s in force
A clause stopping a landlord, association or town from prohibiting a compliant device, usually allowing “reasonable” limits; it is separate from the interconnection exemption. Four signed laws limit landlords (Vermont, Virginia, New Jersey and Colorado); as of 23 September 2026 only Vermont’s protection is in force. See what renters can do.
Solar-access law · 1 of 10 statutes clearly covers a railing
An older kind of statute voiding association rules against solar, written for the roof of a house the owner owns. balco.nyc read ten against a condo or co-op railing panel: one clearly covers it (Vermont’s), two are unclear, seven do not. New York’s, Real Property Law §342, covers only rooftops. See condo, co-op and HOA rules.
Common element (condo) · 3 of 9 laws curb association bans
The parts of a condominium owned by all unit owners together. A balcony and its railing are commonly a limited common element: reserved for one unit, controlled by the association. Three signed laws curb association bans (New Jersey, Vermont and, from 2027, Colorado); Virginia’s expressly preserves them. See why access laws stop at the balcony.
Alteration agreement (co-op) · facade inspections every 5 years
A co-op’s proprietary lease typically requires the board’s written consent for alterations, usually given as an alteration agreement setting conditions for the work. Boards have a reason for caution: New York City buildings over six stories must have their facades, railings and attached equipment included, examined every five years. See co-ops and condos in NYC.

4The energy model

balco.nyc’s estimate is PVWatts output for the panel’s tilt and direction, times a railing factor, times a shade factor, month by month.

Kilowatt (kW) and kilowatt-hour (kWh) · 800 W makes 604 kWh a year (NYC)
A kilowatt is a rate of power; a kilowatt-hour is energy, one kilowatt for one hour, and what the utility bills. balco.nyc’s PVWatts run puts an unshaded, south-facing 800 W railing panel in New York City at 604 kWh a year, 8.8% of an average New York household’s use. See output by state and direction.
Capacity factor · 8.6% for a vertical NYC panel
Energy produced divided by what the system would make at full rated power all year. By balco.nyc’s arithmetic, 604 kWh ÷ (0.8 kW × 8,760 h) = 8.6% in New York City, 14.1% tilted to 35°, and a median 9.4% for a vertical south panel across all 51 state runs. The EIA reports 24.4% for US utility-scale solar in 2025. See why a vertical panel differs.
PVWatts · V8; defaults overstate a railing by 72%
The free hourly solar model from the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR, formerly NREL), which balco.nyc runs for every estimate. At its rooftop defaults it overstates a vertical, south-facing 800 W panel in New York City by about 72% (1,037 kWh against 604), mostly through the 20° default tilt. See what PVWatts gets wrong.
TMY and NSRDB · 8,760 hours
A typical meteorological year is 8,760 hours of weather chosen to represent a multi-year period. PVWatts V8 uses TMY files from the National Solar Radiation Database (NSRDB), NLR’s satellite-derived record of direct and diffuse sunlight, on a 4 km grid. Any real year will differ. See how accurate PVWatts is.
Tilt · 35° makes 64% more than vertical (NYC)
The panel’s angle from horizontal; a railing panel hangs at 90°. balco.nyc models 35°, 60°, 70° and 90°. In New York, tilting a south-facing panel from vertical to 35° raises output from 604 to 988 kWh a year, because a vertical panel sees only half the sky and meets the summer sun at a glancing angle. See railing versus roof.
Azimuth · north makes 72% less than south (NYC)
The compass direction a panel faces, in degrees clockwise from true north: 90° east, 180° south. In balco.nyc’s New York runs a vertical panel facing east makes 26% less than one facing south, west 30% less and north 72% less. Manhattan’s street grid runs about 29° off true north. See east, west and north.
Railing factor · 0.95 for a vertical mount
balco.nyc’s multiplier for light the railing, brackets and slab above take off a panel’s lower edge, which PVWatts cannot see; an assumption, not a measurement. It is 0.95 at 90°, 0.97 at 70°, 0.98 at 60° and 0.99 at 35°, turning New York’s 635.7 kWh PVWatts result into 604. See where it enters the chain.
Soiling · 3–7% a month, 5% on average
Light lost to dust, pollen and grime on the glass. PVWatts’ default 14% losses include 2% for it; balco.nyc models soiling separately, month by month, from 3% in winter to 7% in pollen season, 5% on average, and keeps 14% for wiring and other losses. See the methodology.
Beam and diffuse light · 60/40 split
Beam light comes straight from the sun and casts shadows; diffuse light is scattered by air and cloud and comes from the whole sky. balco.nyc’s shade model splits light 60% beam and 40% diffuse, in line with the Northeast’s diffuse share of about 35–42%, which is why a north-facing panel does not produce zero. See diffuse light.
Horizon profile · 360 one-degree bins
The skyline seen from the balcony: the highest obstruction angle in each direction. balco.nyc builds it in 360 one-degree bins, in New York City from every building within 200 m, elsewhere from your description of what stands opposite. When the sun is below the profile, there is no beam light. See how the shade step works.
Shade factor · 1.00 open; 0.38 on a low NYC floor
The share of the light PVWatts assumed that reaches the balcony. It prices obstruction only, so an open view scores 1.00 in any direction; monthly values are floored at 0.10. A third-floor New York balcony facing a six-to-ten-story building 20 m away scores an effective 0.38, cutting 604 kWh a year to 227. See the worked examples.

5Money

Savings are kilowatt-hours times the price of a kilowatt-hour you no longer buy; most disagreements between calculators come down to which price.

Self-consumption · 60% at a 150 W baseload (NYC)
The share of output used in the home as it is produced; with exports unpaid, only that share saves money. balco.nyc’s hourly New York run finds an apartment drawing a steady 150 W uses about 60% of an 800 W panel’s 604 kWh, saving $124 a year rather than $205. See how much an apartment uses.
Marginal rate vs average price · 34¢ (Con Edison) vs 27.96¢ (NY average)
The marginal rate is what one kWh more or less costs, excluding fixed charges; the average price divides the whole bill, fixed charges included, by kWh. Solar saves at the margin. balco.nyc uses Con Edison’s 34¢ in New York City and EIA state averages elsewhere; New York State’s, 27.96¢, blends in lower-priced utilities outside the city. See what a kWh costs.
Price to compare · supply only, about 13–18¢
The utility’s supply-only price per kWh, published for comparing independent suppliers (ESCOs in New York). Reported Con Edison figures for 2026 run about 13–18¢: right for choosing a supplier, too low for valuing solar you use, which also avoids delivery charges, surcharges and tax. See supply-only rates.
Fixed customer charge · $20 a month set aside (NYC)
A charge billed every month regardless of use, for costs such as billing and meter reading. Solar cannot reduce it, so balco.nyc subtracts $20 from a New York City bill before converting it to kWh. Elsewhere the EIA average spreads fixed charges over every kWh, which can overstate savings. See why it is left out.
Payback (nominal) · 5.5 years in NYC; 9.9 in the median state
Years until cumulative savings equal the kit’s cost. balco.nyc’s figure compounds rate escalation and degradation with no discount rate, so it is nominal. On a $1,200 kit an unshaded south-facing railing pays back in 5.5 years in New York City (5.8 simple) and 9.9 in the median state’s largest city. See payback in every state.
Degradation · 0.5% a year; 88.7% left in year 25
The slow loss of a panel’s output with age. A review by NREL researchers of more than 11,000 measured rates found a median of 0.5–0.6% a year for crystalline silicon. balco.nyc uses 0.5% for mid-tier equipment, leaving 88.7% of the original output in year 25. See the methodology.
Escalation · 3% a year by default
The assumed yearly rise in electricity prices, which raises the value of future kWh. balco.nyc defaults to 3%, with 2% and 4% presets. By its arithmetic, the unshaded New York City panel’s 25-year nominal savings are $7,000 at 3%, $6,165 at 2% and $7,976 at 4%. See reading the 25-year number.

6The grid and emissions

What happens to power the apartment does not use, and what the power it does use displaces.

Net metering · no signed plug-in law pays for exports
Billing that credits electricity sent to the grid against electricity bought; it normally comes with the paperwork plug-in laws waive. balco.nyc’s reading of the nine signed laws finds none requiring payment for plug-in exports, and at least five expressly excluding the devices from net metering. See surplus power.
Export compensation · none; 240 kWh a year given away (NYC)
Payment or credit for surplus power leaving through the meter. balco.nyc assumes none unless a law or tariff provides it; New York’s SUNNY Act bill says exports “shall be uncompensated”. In the site’s hourly New York run, an apartment drawing a steady 150 W gives away 240 kWh a year of an 800 W panel’s output. See surplus is given away.
eGRID subregion · 27 regions; NYC’s rate is 3.6× upstate’s
One of 27 grid regions in the EPA’s eGRID database, each with its own plant mix and emission rates; balco.nyc finds an address’s subregion from its ZIP code. New York City and Westchester (NYCW), 98% gas-fired, emit 864.5 lb of CO₂ per MWh, 3.6 times upstate New York’s 242.1. See CO₂ in the calculation.
CO₂ output emission rate · 0.864 lb per kWh (NYC)
Pounds of CO₂ per unit of electricity generated in a subregion, averaged over all its plants. balco.nyc uses eGRID2023’s total output rate: 604 kWh × 0.864 lb is about 522 lb a year in New York City. The non-baseload rate, 976.9 lb/MWh in NYCW, would give a larger figure. See the methodology.

7Common questions

What is a microinverter in balcony solar?

A microinverter is the small inverter that turns a solar panel’s direct current into alternating current synchronized to the grid, so the power can flow into a home’s circuits through a plug. US plug-in laws cap its AC output, most often at 1,200 W.

What is anti-islanding?

Anti-islanding is the inverter function that stops a solar system from energizing wires after the grid goes down, which could shock a utility worker. Under UL 1741 and IEEE 1547 an inverter must stop within 2 seconds of losing the grid, which is also why a plug-in panel gives no power in a blackout.

What does kWh mean for a solar panel?

A kilowatt-hour is energy: one kilowatt for one hour, the unit a utility bills. A panel’s watt rating says how fast it can produce; its annual kWh says how much it does. balco.nyc puts an unshaded, south-facing 800 W railing panel in New York City at 604 kWh a year.

Are balcony solar and plug-in solar the same thing?

Yes. Both describe a small solar system whose microinverter plugs into a household outlet and feeds the home’s circuits; the German name is Balkonkraftwerk. Germany caps these devices at 800 VA of inverter output, and eight of the nine signed US state laws cap them at 1,200 W.

What is the capacity factor of a balcony solar panel?

By balco.nyc’s arithmetic, an unshaded south-facing 800 W railing panel in New York City has a capacity factor of 8.6%, and the median across the largest city in every state and DC is 9.4%, against 24.4% for US utility-scale solar in 2025. Shade lowers a balcony’s figure further.

Is UL 3700 a standard?

Not a consensus standard. UL 3700 is UL LLC’s Outline of Investigation for Interactive Plug-In PV Equipment and Systems, edition 1, dated 11 December 2025. As of 23 September 2026 no complete plug-in system is listed to it, though a few microinverters claim certification as components.

About balco.nyc Methodology llms.txt

balco.nyc is an independent, free balcony-solar calculator: it estimates what a plug-in solar panel would produce and save at any US address, with a 3D shade model for New York City buildings. Not affiliated with the City of New York, Con Edison, NYSERDA, NREL, the EIA, the EPA, or any seller of solar equipment. NYC results carry a modeled uncertainty range of about 15%. Plug-in solar rules vary by state; in New York the SUNNY Act has passed both chambers and awaits the Governor's signature.
