Renters and the law

Can renters install balcony solar?

Usually only with the landlord’s permission. As of 23 September 2026, four states — Vermont, Virginia, New Jersey and Colorado — have signed laws that limit a landlord’s power to refuse, and only Vermont’s protection is in force; the other three start in January or March 2027. balco.nyc’s count of Census data puts 3.3 million renter households, 7.2% of the US total, in those four states, and 76,431 (0.17%) in the one where the protection works today. New York’s SUNNY Act has passed both chambers and awaits the Governor's signature, and it grants no right to install.

1The short answer, and the four exceptions

A plug-in kit hangs on a railing the landlord owns and plugs into wiring the landlord owns, so in most of the country the lease and the landlord decide. A law that makes plug-in solar legal is not one that makes it your right: five of the nine signed US plug-in laws stop the utility from demanding approval and give tenants nothing.

balco.nyc read the enacted text of every law in its state tracker. Four limit a landlord’s power to refuse: Vermont (in force since 1 July 2026; after 10 days’ notice with no answer, the tenant may proceed), Virginia (from 1 January 2027; landlords who own more than four rental units), New Jersey (from 1 March 2027; tenants who pay their own electric bill) and Colorado (from 1 January 2027; no person may “unreasonably prohibit” a device). All four still let the landlord impose reasonable restrictions.

balco.nyc’s count from the Census Bureau’s 2024 American Community Survey puts 3,334,151 renter households in those four states, 7.2% of the 46.1 million in the United States. Until the 2027 start dates, the protected group is Vermont’s 76,431 renter households: 0.17% of American renters. New York alone has 3,575,503 renter households, more than the four states combined, and its SUNNY Act would give none of them a right to install. Everyone else needs permission.

Not legal advice. This page summarizes statutes and bills as of 23 September 2026. Your lease, your state’s law and any association rules decide your case, and a law that is signed but not yet in effect protects nobody.

Laws with nothing for tenants. Utah, Maryland, Connecticut, New Hampshire and Maine have signed plug-in laws with no tenant right; Maine’s only requires anyone installing on a structure they do not own to protect it and restore it on removal, a duty rather than a right. The same silence runs through New York’s SUNNY Act, which has passed both chambers and awaits the Governor's signature; California’s SB 868, awaiting the Governor’s decision; and pending bills in Massachusetts, Michigan, Pennsylvania and the District of Columbia. Tracker summaries saying the Massachusetts and Pennsylvania bills protect tenants describe provisions balco.nyc could not find in either text.

3How many renters the laws reach

A law’s reach is a head count, so balco.nyc made one: renter-occupied homes (households) by state from the Census Bureau’s 2024 American Community Survey 1-year estimates, table B25003 (Tenure). That is the newest 1-year release; on 4 August 2026 the Bureau said the 2025 release date “is being determined.” Two laws cover only some tenants, so each gets a narrower estimate:

  • Virginia turns on how many units the landlord owns, which no Census table records. The nearest proxy is renters in buildings of five or more units (table B25032): 555,373, or 49.0% of the state’s renters.
  • New Jersey turns on paying your own electric bill. The published tables do not split electricity billing by tenure, but B25069 records renters who pay extra for at least one utility: 1,152,187, against 126,937 who pay for none. The first figure is a ceiling.

The arithmetic: 76,431 + 844,547 + 1,134,049 + 1,279,124 = 3,334,151, and 3,334,151 ÷ 46,101,640 = 7.2%; the narrower column sums to 2,628,538, or 5.7%. The Census margin of error on the four-state total is about ±25,000 at 90% confidence. For scale, New York has 3,575,503 renter households (45.7% of its households), and the five states with a plug-in law but no tenant clause hold 1,939,324 renter households for whom plugging in is, or will shortly be, legal and the landlord still decides.

The same data qualifies a figure in New Jersey’s signing announcement, where an advocate says the law benefits “roughly 40 percent of New Jerseyans who rent or live in apartments.” That counts people and includes apartment owners, who fall under the law’s association provisions, not its tenant clause. By households, the ACS puts renters at 36.1% of the state’s total (1,279,124 of 3,543,944), and the tenant clause reaches at most 32.5% (1,152,187).

What these numbers are not: they count households, not balconies. The ACS does not ask whether a home has a balcony, a patio or an outdoor outlet, so the renters who could actually use these protections are fewer than every figure above, by an amount nobody has measured. The Virginia proxy is loose both ways: it includes condo units rented out by small owners, who are not covered, and units on ratio utility billing, which the law excludes; it misses houses owned by large landlords, who are covered.

4What “can’t prohibit” still allows

None of the four laws hands the balcony to the tenant. Read closely, they replace a landlord’s veto with a landlord’s conditions:

  • Reasonable restrictions survive everywhere. Virginia names “size, place, and manner of placement” and lets the landlord bar devices anywhere but the exterior of the tenant’s premises; Colorado lists notice, fire and overload risk, damage and location. What is reasonable has not been tested in any of the four states.
  • Notice comes first. Seven days in Virginia, with documentation that the device qualifies and where it will go; ten in Vermont; fourteen, in writing, in New Jersey. The laws protect a tenant who gives notice before installing.
  • Wiring stays the landlord’s. Vermont says a landlord “shall not be compelled to perform or pay for electrical work”; Virginia requires express written approval for anything that alters wiring or panels. If your balcony has no outdoor outlet, none of these laws gets you one.
  • Damage is the tenant’s. Virginia makes the tenant responsible for any damage the device causes.
  • Only a qualifying device is protected. Vermont covers devices certified to UL 3700 “or an equivalent certification.” No complete plug-in system is certified to UL 3700 as of September 2026 (components are), so a Vermont tenant’s protection depends on whether a kit’s listing counts as equivalent, which the Act does not settle. New Jersey also accepts a comparable standard; Virginia and Colorado ask for certification or listing by a nationally recognized testing laboratory.
  • Surplus power earns nothing. Vermont says exports “shall not be compensated,” Virginia excludes the devices from net metering, and New Jersey and Colorado are silent. Assume what you do not use is given away.
  • Association rules may still bind. Virginia’s law does not override condominium or HOA documents, so a tenant in a rented condo can be protected against the landlord and still refused by the association; New Jersey’s and Colorado’s bind associations too. See condos, co-ops and HOAs.

5Asking your landlord: a checklist and a letter

In most states the request is the whole process; in the four above it doubles as the statutory notice. A specific written request is easier to approve than a general one. Do the physical checks first with the installation guide; if the landlord’s worry is fire or shock, the safety evidence is summarized separately. Include:

  • The device: make, model, panel count, maximum AC output, and the certification exactly as the listing states it, including the anti-islanding feature each of the four laws requires.
  • Size and weight of the panels with brackets.
  • Mounting without drilling: clamps or hooks inside the railing, nothing fixed to the facade, secured against wind.
  • The outlet: which existing outdoor outlet, whether it is GFCI-protected and covered, no extension cord, and no new wiring without written approval.
  • Insurance: your renters policy’s liability limit, and acceptance of responsibility for damage.
  • Removal at move-out, or on short notice for building work, with the railing restored.
  • The law, if one applies: the statute and the notice period.

A neutral template.

Dear [landlord or managing agent],

I would like to install a small plug-in solar system on the balcony of [unit]. The details are below, and I am happy to discuss any conditions.

Device: [make and model], [number] panels, [watts] maximum AC output, listed to [standard] by [laboratory]. It switches off automatically if grid power is lost. Specification sheet attached.

Size and mounting: [dimensions], [total weight]. It clamps to the inside of the railing; nothing is drilled into or fixed to the building.

Power: it plugs into the existing outdoor outlet at [location]. No extension cord and no change to the wiring.

Insurance: my renters policy with [insurer] carries [amount] of liability coverage, and I will be responsible for any damage the system causes.

Removal: I will remove it and restore the railing when I move out, or within [number] days if you need access for building work.

[If a state law applies: I am giving this notice under [statute], [number] days before installation on [date].]

I would be grateful for your written approval, or for any conditions you would attach.

The template is a starting point, not legal advice. Where a statute applies, match its wording: Virginia’s notice, for example, must include documentation that the device meets the law’s definition and identify the proposed location.

6If the answer is no

Outside the four states, a refusal usually stands until the lease changes. A no to a vague request is sometimes a yes to a specific one: a smaller system, a floor stand instead of a railing mount, proof of insurance, a firm removal commitment. Lease renewal is a natural moment to ask again.

Where one of the four laws covers you, the landlord can restrict but not refuse, and a dispute over what is “reasonable” goes through the ordinary landlord-tenant channels. In New York the SUNNY Act has passed both chambers and awaits the Governor's signature, and nothing in it limits a landlord’s answer: renters will need permission after it is signed, as they do now.

Before asking at all, check it is worth asking. Output depends mostly on how much sky the balcony sees, which is a kilowatt-hour number, not a yes or no; the calculator gives it for any US address, and a shaded balcony may not pay back in the time you expect to stay.

7Common questions

Can my landlord stop me from installing balcony solar?

In most US states, yes: a plug-in kit attaches to a railing and outlet the landlord owns, so the lease and the landlord decide. As of September 2026 four states have signed laws limiting that power. Vermont's protection is in force; Virginia's and Colorado's start on 1 January 2027 and New Jersey's on 1 March 2027. Even there, the landlord may impose reasonable restrictions.

Which states give renters a right to install plug-in solar?

Vermont (in force since 1 July 2026, 10 days' notice), Virginia (from 1 January 2027, tenants of landlords owning more than four rental units, excluding ratio-utility-billed units), New Jersey (from 1 March 2027, tenants who pay their own electric bill, 14 days' written notice) and Colorado (from 1 January 2027, no unreasonable prohibition). Tenant bills in Illinois and North Carolina are pending but stalled.

Does New York's SUNNY Act let renters install balcony solar?

No. The SUNNY Act (S8512C / A9111C) has passed both chambers and awaits the Governor's signature, and it contains no landlord or tenant provision. A New York renter would still need the landlord's permission after it is signed.

How many renters do state balcony solar tenant protections cover?

balco.nyc's count from the Census Bureau's 2024 American Community Survey (table B25003) puts 3,334,151 renter households, 7.2% of the US total, in Vermont, Virginia, New Jersey and Colorado; about 2.6 million (5.7%) narrowed to the tenants each law covers. Until 2027 only Vermont's 76,431, or 0.17%, are protected.

Does my landlord have to add an outdoor outlet for balcony solar?

No. None of the four state laws with tenant provisions requires a landlord to add wiring. Vermont's says a landlord shall not be compelled to perform or pay for electrical work, and Virginia's requires the landlord's express written approval for anything that alters wiring or electrical panels.

What should a balcony solar request to a landlord include?

The device's make, model, AC wattage and certification; its size and weight; how it mounts without drilling; which existing outdoor outlet it uses, with no extension cord; your renters insurance and acceptance of responsibility for damage; removal and restoration at move-out; and, where a state tenant law applies, the statute and notice period.
