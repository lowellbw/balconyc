Rates

How to find what a kWh really costs you, from your electric bill

What a kWh really costs you is the sum of every charge on your bill that is billed per kilowatt-hour — supply, delivery, riders and surcharges, plus the taxes on them — with the fixed monthly charge left out. That marginal rate is what each kWh a balcony panel replaces is worth. Working from the published tariffs of five large utilities, balco.nyc found it runs from 13.3¢ at Florida Power & Light to 37.5¢ at PG&E for a south-facing railing’s output, and that the EIA state average, the calculator’s default outside New York City, misses it by between 12% too low and 16% too high.

1Average, marginal and the price to compare

There are at least three ways to put a price on a kilowatt-hour, and they answer different questions.

  • Average price: the whole bill divided by the kWh on it. It includes the fixed monthly charge, so it rises as your usage falls. The EIA state figures balco.nyc’s calculator uses outside New York City are this kind: every utility’s residential revenue in the state, which EIA’s instructions say includes customer charges, surcharges and franchise fees, divided by kWh sold over the twelve months to June 2026.
  • Price to compare: the supply part only (generation and, in some states, transmission), published so you can compare offers from retail suppliers. ComEd’s is 10.399¢ for June to September 2026 and PECO’s is 11.759¢ for June to November 2026. By balco.nyc’s computation below, those are 61% and 53% of what a kWh actually costs those customers.
  • Marginal price: what one kWh more, or one fewer, changes on the bill. That is every charge billed per kWh, plus the taxes on them, and nothing that is fixed.

A balcony panel only changes the third. It reduces the kWh you draw from the grid, not the customer charge, which you pay whether you use 50 kWh or 5,000.

The rule: value solar you use yourself at the marginal price. The average spreads a fixed charge you cannot avoid over every kWh; the price to compare leaves out delivery, which you still avoid.

New York City, where balco.nyc uses Con Edison’s all-in marginal rate of about 34¢, has its own page. This one is for everywhere else.

2How to work out your marginal rate from one bill

You need one recent bill that lists each charge with its rate. Then:

  1. Find every charge billed per kWh: lines shown as “kWh × rate”. Supply or generation, fuel, transmission, distribution or delivery, and riders with names like conservation, environmental, storm protection or energy efficiency. Add up their rates.
  2. Leave out anything fixed: the customer, basic service, base services or meter charge, and any minimum-bill top-up. Leave out one-off credits too, such as Florida Power & Light’s one-month storm refund in September 2026 or California’s Climate Credit, a flat $36.18 per PG&E household.
  3. Keep per-kWh credits that recur. ComEd’s carbon-free resource adjustment is billed per kWh and was a credit in every month of ComEd’s filings from June 2025 to January 2026, so it belongs in the sum with a minus sign.
  4. Check for tiers. If the rate changes with how much you use, your marginal price is the rate for the block your last kWh lands in, not the first block.
  5. Add the taxes that scale with usage: taxes charged per kWh, and percentage taxes and fees (gross receipts, franchise, sales tax) applied to the per-kWh total.

Florida Power & Light’s own sample bill for a 1,000 kWh customer in September 2026 shows how it works. The per-kWh lines are base energy $78.65, fuel $28.93, storm protection $9.95, environmental $3.45, conservation $1.48, capacity $0.52 and a transition credit of −$0.40: $122.58, or 12.26¢ a kWh. Gross receipts tax and the regulatory fee add 2.65%, which gives 12.58¢. The $10.52 base charge and the $7.97 storm refund are left out. The bill’s own total, divided by 1,000, is 12.85¢; in a month without the refund it would be 13.66¢. Neither is what a kWh costs.

The shortcut on the NYC page, total minus the fixed charge divided by kWh, gives the right answer only when every kWh is priced the same. FPL’s is not: above 1,000 kWh a month its energy and fuel charges each rise by 1¢, and the marginal price becomes 14.64¢.

Three things stand out. None of the fixed charges, from $10.52 to $24.13 a month, moves when you use less. At PG&E, FPL and Georgia Power the price of a kWh changes with the season, with how much you use, or both; ComEd’s summer and winter supply prices differ only slightly, and PECO’s changes each June and December. And supply is only a little over half the price: at ComEd and PECO, delivery, riders and taxes add another 6.9¢ and 10.3¢ to every kWh.

What the computation leaves out:

  • ComEd’s carbon-free resource adjustment is reset monthly: a 2.38¢ credit in January 2026 in ComEd’s filing, roughly a penny in June 2026 according to the Citizens Utility Board. We found no published September value, so the table stops before it; subtract what your bill shows. The purchased electricity adjustment, also monthly, is included at the Illinois Commerce Commission’s September figure, −0.087¢.
  • Local taxes that vary by city: California’s utility users taxes, Florida’s franchise fees and municipal utility taxes, and Georgia’s sales tax, which the Georgia Public Service Commission’s bill calculator leaves you to enter. Where they apply, your rate is higher than the table.
  • Other supply: customers of a community choice aggregator or a retail supplier pay their provider’s price instead of the utility’s generation charge.
  • ComEd’s franchise cost uses ComEd’s system-average 3.538%; Chicago’s may differ. Single-family homes pay a higher distribution charge, 6.23¢, for a marginal rate of 18.7¢.

4How far off the state average is, and which way

What matters is the rate on the kWh a panel actually replaces. A panel hung vertically on a south-facing railing faces the low winter sun almost head-on: in balco.nyc’s PVWatts runs for the largest city in every state, it makes a median of 75% of its output from October to May, from 58% in Anchorage to 82% in Honolulu. Where a tariff is seasonal, balco.nyc weighted it by the railing’s month-by-month output, after the site’s 0.95 railing factor: the Los Angeles run for PG&E, the nearest in the site’s data, with 78% of output from October to May, and Atlanta for Georgia Power, 77%. For FPL’s 1,000 kWh threshold, EIA’s monthly data show the average Florida home used more than 1,000 kWh in six of the twelve months to June 2026, May to October; the Jacksonville railing makes 36% of its output in those months.

The state average is not biased in one direction: too low for three utilities, too high for two. Each gap has a traceable cause.

  • Fixed charges push the average up. A ComEd apartment customer at Illinois’s average of 693 kWh a month pays about 19.6¢ a kWh on average against 17.2¢ at the margin; at 300 kWh the average is 22.9¢. With ComEd’s carbon-free credit subtracted, the state average overstates the apartment’s rate further still.
  • Tiers can push the average down. At Florida’s average of 1,104 kWh, an FPL bill averages 13.8¢, below the 14.6¢ marginal rate. But a south railing makes most of its power in winter, when the typical Florida home stays under 1,000 kWh and the marginal rate is 12.6¢. The Florida average also includes franchise fees, which the table leaves out, and every other utility in the state.
  • A state average mixes utilities. California’s blends PG&E with every other utility in the state and with customers on discounted low-income rates. PG&E customers on its standard tiered plan, E-1, pay 40.7¢ above baseline, higher still.
  • The average is behind. PECO’s price to compare rose from 11.024¢ to 11.759¢ on 1 June 2026, after the EIA window closed. At the old price the marginal rate would be 21.3¢, still 0.8¢ above the state average.
  • Seasons can cancel out. Georgia Power’s summer kWh cost up to 21.7¢, its winter kWh 13.6¢, and the railing’s winter-heavy output lands close to the state average.

The error passes straight into the result: first-year savings are output times rate, so a rate 12% too low understates them by 12%. Price already explains most of the difference in payback between states; it deserves the five minutes this takes.

5Tiers, seasons and time of use: which kWh a railing replaces

Tiers. A panel takes kWh off the top of your monthly usage, not the bottom. Where the price rises with usage (FPL above 1,000 kWh, Georgia Power in summer, PG&E above baseline), the panel saves the top block’s price in months you reach it and the lower price in months you do not. If you never leave the first block, the first block is your marginal rate: a small PG&E user within baseline saves 32.6¢ on the E-1 plan, not 40.7¢.

Seasons. Georgia Power charges 13.6¢ a kWh from October to May and up to 21.7¢ in summer. Because a south railing makes 77% of its Atlanta output in the cheap season, its kWh are worth 15.5¢ on average. East- and west-facing railings make 60% to 62% of their Atlanta output from October to May, so their kWh are worth more, 16.7¢ to 16.9¢, though they make about a fifth less power. How output splits between winter and summer is set out by state and direction.

Time of use. PG&E’s E-TOU-C plan charges its peak price from 4 to 9 p.m. every day. A south-facing railing makes most of its output around midday, so it replaces off-peak kWh, 36.8¢ to 40.0¢, rather than peak kWh at up to 52.3¢. PECO’s optional time-of-use supply price puts the peak earlier: from June to November 2026 it is 32.404¢ from 2 to 6 p.m. on weekdays and 9.336¢ at most other hours. On that plan, each kWh from a west-facing railing’s afternoon output is worth more than one from a south railing at midday.

All of this applies only to kWh you use at home. Power the panel sends back to the grid is almost always unpaid, so the share you use yourself matters as much as the rate: how much of a panel’s output an apartment uses.

6Which number to type into the calculator

On the calculator’s results page, open Adjust assumptions and find the box labeled Electricity rate (¢/kWh). Left blank, it uses Con Edison’s 34¢ marginal rate in New York City and, everywhere else, your state’s EIA average, which includes fixed charges and every utility in the state. Savings are output multiplied by this number, so replace the default with your own.

  • One price all year: type the sum from your bill, as in the steps above.
  • Seasonal or tiered prices: weight them by when the panel produces. For a south railing, three-quarters winter (October to May) and one-quarter summer is within five points of balco.nyc’s runs in 45 of 51 cities; use the rate of the block your usage actually reaches.
  • Time of use: use the off-peak rate for a south railing. For an east- or west-facing railing, check how much of your plan’s peak window falls in its sunny hours.
  • Retail supplier or community choice: use your contract’s supply price in place of the utility’s generation charge, plus the utility’s delivery charges, riders and taxes, which you pay regardless.

Then run the calculator with it. The number will not stay put: ComEd’s supply price resets on 1 October, PECO’s on 1 December, and fuel and rider charges move through the year. The calculator’s rate escalation setting (3% a year by default) covers the long run; the figure you type should be today’s.

7Common questions

How do I find the cost per kWh on my electric bill?

Add up the rates of every charge billed per kilowatt-hour: supply or generation, fuel, transmission, distribution or delivery, and the per-kWh riders and surcharges. Then add the taxes and fees that scale with usage. Leave out the fixed monthly customer or meter charge and any one-off credits. If the price changes with usage, use the rate for the block your last kWh falls in.

What is the difference between my average and my marginal electricity rate?

The average rate is your whole bill divided by the kWh on it, so it includes the fixed monthly charge and rises as your usage falls. The marginal rate is what one kWh more or less changes on the bill. For a ComEd apartment customer in Chicago using 693 kWh a month, balco.nyc computed an average of about 19.6 cents against a marginal rate of 17.2 cents in September 2026, before ComEd's monthly carbon-free credit.

Is the price to compare my electricity rate?

No. The price to compare covers supply only, so you can judge offers from retail suppliers. You also pay delivery charges, riders and taxes on every kWh. In balco.nyc's September 2026 computation, ComEd's price to compare of 10.399 cents was 61% of the all-in marginal rate, and PECO's 11.759 cents was 53%.

Should I use my state's average electricity price to estimate solar savings?

Only as a starting point. balco.nyc compared EIA state averages with the marginal rate of five large utilities, worked from their published tariffs and weighted to a south-facing railing's output. The average ranged from 12% too low (PG&E, California) to 16% too high (Florida Power & Light), so savings based on it were off by the same proportions.

Which rate should I use if my utility has tiers or time-of-use pricing?

Use the rate for the kWh the panel replaces. Under tiers, that is the top block you reach in the months the panel produces; a south-facing railing makes about three-quarters of its output from October to May. Under time-of-use pricing, a south railing replaces mostly midday kWh, so the off-peak rate usually applies unless the peak window starts in the early afternoon.

Does a balcony solar panel lower the fixed charge on my bill?

No. Customer, meter and base services charges are the same whether you use 50 kWh or 5,000, so solar cannot reduce them. That is why a solar kWh is worth the marginal rate, which leaves the fixed charge out, and why using an average rate that includes it overstates savings for most small households.

balco.nyc is an independent, free balcony-solar calculator: it estimates what a plug-in solar panel would produce and save at any US address, with a 3D shade model for New York City buildings. Not affiliated with the City of New York, Con Edison, NYSERDA, NREL, the EIA, the EPA, or any seller of solar equipment. NYC results carry a modeled uncertainty range of about 15%. Plug-in solar rules vary by state; in New York the SUNNY Act has passed both chambers and awaits the Governor's signature.
