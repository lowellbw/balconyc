The calculation

How to calculate balcony solar output and savings for any US address

Take NREL’s PVWatts output for your location, tilt and direction. Multiply it by 0.95 for the railing and by a month-by-month shade factor for whatever stands opposite, then value each kWh at the price you actually stop paying. Worked through that chain with balco.nyc’s own PVWatts runs, an 800 W south-facing panel on a vertical railing makes 778 kWh a year in Phoenix, 616 in Chicago and 604 in New York City with an open view, and 328, 239 and 227 kWh on a third floor facing a six-to-ten-story building across a 20 m street. Shade changes the kWh more than the city does, and the price of a kWh decides the payback.

1The chain in one line, and what it gives for three cities

Every balcony solar estimate, including the one the balco.nyc calculator gives you, is a few multiplications and one loop:

  • kWh a year = the sum over twelve months of (PVWatts monthly output × railing factor × that month’s shade factor)
  • Savings a year = kWh × the price of a kWh you no longer buy
  • Payback = years until cumulative savings reach the kit cost, with the price rising 3% and output falling 0.5% a year
  • CO₂ avoided = kWh × your grid region’s emission rate

That is six steps, each with a number and a source, set out below so you can run the chain by hand or check another calculator against it. To show what it does, balco.nyc ran it for an 800 W system on a vertical, south-facing railing in three cities with very different sun and prices, using the site’s own PVWatts runs for each state’s largest city and its own shade code:

  • With an open view the panel makes about 778 kWh a year in Phoenix, 616 in Chicago and 604 in New York City. On a railing, Phoenix’s desert sun buys only 29% more than New York’s, against 37% more at a roof-style 35° tilt, because a vertical panel meets the high summer sun at a glancing angle.
  • From a third floor facing a six-to-ten-story building across a 20 m street, the same panel loses 58% to 62% of that: 328, 239 and 227 kWh. What stands opposite matters more than which city you are in.
  • Price matters more than sun. At Con Edison’s 34¢ marginal rate the open New York panel saves $205 a year. At Arizona’s 15.4¢ average the Phoenix panel makes 29% more electricity and saves $120.

These are modeled figures for one reference point per city, not measurements. The full table is in section 5.

2Step 1: the sun and the panel’s geometry (PVWatts V8)

The first number is the one everything else multiplies: what the panel would make at this spot, tilt and direction with nothing in the way. balco.nyc gets it from NREL’s PVWatts V8, the Department of Energy’s reference production model, which simulates every hour of a typical meteorological year (TMY) drawn from the satellite-derived National Solar Radiation Database. One caveat: PVWatts’s own manual builds its 14% default partly from 2% soiling and 3% shading, and balco.nyc applies soiling and shading again separately. Any overlap pushes the estimate down, not up.

For an 800 W vertical south panel PVWatts returns 819.4 kWh a year in Phoenix, 648.8 in Chicago and 635.7 in New York City, before any railing loss. Tilted to 35° the same panel would make 1,369, 1,010 and 998 kWh, so a vertical railing keeps only 60% to 64% of a rooftop-style figure. That ratio explains most inflated balcony estimates: they quote a tilted panel’s output for one that will hang straight down.

The monthly shape matters too. A vertical south panel is a winter machine: it meets the low winter sun almost head-on. In Phoenix it peaks at 104.7 kWh in December and bottoms out at 26.5 in June; in New York it runs from 73.0 in January to 31.9 in June. Winter is also when buildings opposite do the most damage. And a TMY is a typical year, not a forecast; any real year will differ.

3Steps 2 and 3: the railing, and the building across the street

Step 2, the railing. The railing, the mounting hardware and the slab above clip a rail-mounted panel’s lower edge, and PVWatts cannot see them. balco.nyc multiplies by 0.95 for a vertical panel (0.97 at 70°, 0.98 at 60°, 0.99 at 35°). It is the site’s own assumption, not a measurement, and it applies to every estimate. Open-view output is therefore 819.4 × 0.95 = 778 kWh in Phoenix, 616 in Chicago and 604 in New York.

Step 3, the buildings opposite. PVWatts assumes an open horizon, so the shade model answers one question: of the light PVWatts assumed, how much reaches this balcony? It starts from a horizon profile, the highest obstruction angle in each of 360 compass directions. In New York City the profile comes from real building footprints and heights within 200 m. Elsewhere it comes from your description, modeled as a continuous wall parallel to your facade:

β(φ) = atan( (H − h) × cos φ / D ), with h = (floor − 1) × 3.05 m + 1.0 m

H is the height of the building opposite, h your balcony’s height, D the distance and φ the angle away from straight out. In the worked examples a third floor gives h = 7.1 m, “6 to 10 floors” is H = 25 m, and “across a street” is D = 20 m. Straight out, the wall rises to atan(17.9 / 20) = 41.8° (32.3° at 45° either side). The winter-solstice noon sun reaches only about 33° in Phoenix, 25° in Chicago and 26° in New York. It clears 41.8° at noon only from about early March to early October in Chicago and New York, and early February to early November in Phoenix.

The shade factor comes from a sweep: on the 15th of each month, every 10 minutes from sunrise to sunset, light is split into beam (0.60) and diffuse (0.40). Beam counts only when the sun is above the horizon profile in its direction. Diffuse counts in proportion to the sky the panel can still see, which this wall cuts to 33%:

shade factor = Σ received / Σ assumed, where received = beam × (sun visible) + diffuse × sky openness, and assumed = beam + diffuse

Hours with the sun behind the facade add almost nothing to either sum, since PVWatts has already priced orientation, so an open view returns 1.00 at every orientation, north included. Behind this wall New York’s factor is 0.73 in March, 0.54 in June and 0.72 in September. From October through February it falls to 0.10–0.12, with the sun below the wall all day. Three of those months sit at or just above the model’s floor of 0.10.

Apply the factors month by month, never as one annual average, because the shade is worst when a vertical panel produces most. In Phoenix the panel makes 373 kWh from November to February with an open view and 74 behind this wall: two-thirds of the year’s loss in four months. Multiplying by the plain average of Phoenix’s twelve factors (0.46) would overstate its shaded output by 10%. The month-by-month result is equivalent to 0.42 in Phoenix, 0.39 in Chicago and 0.38 in New York.

4Steps 4 to 6: the price of a kWh, payback and carbon

Step 4, what a kWh is worth. balco.nyc assumes every kWh is used at home, replacing one you would have bought. Over a year the volume is small: 604 kWh is under 9% of the average New York household’s 6,854 kWh. But self-consumption is decided hour by hour. When the panel makes more than the apartment is drawing, the surplus goes to the grid and is assumed to earn nothing unless your state or utility credits it, so the savings figure is a ceiling, not an expectation. Whether you may connect a panel at all varies by state and utility (see the state pages). None of this is legal advice.

The right price is the marginal one: what one fewer kWh takes off your bill, meaning supply, delivery, surcharges and tax but not the fixed monthly charge. In New York City balco.nyc knows the tariff, so it uses Con Edison’s 34¢/kWh all-in marginal rate. That starts from Con Edison’s own SC-1 bill history (33.83¢ in 2025) and is carried into 2026 here. Elsewhere it uses the state’s average residential price, which balco.nyc computes from EIA revenue and sales over the 12 months to June 2026: 15.40¢ in Arizona, 18.54¢ in Illinois, 27.96¢ in New York. An average spreads fixed charges over every kWh, so it can overstate what an avoided kWh saves, and on tiered or time-of-use tariffs it can miss either way. It also blends utilities: New York’s statewide figure sits about six cents below Con Edison’s marginal rate. The best number is your own: a recent bill’s total minus fixed charges, divided by kWh billed. Enter it under Adjust assumptions, or as rate in a link.

Step 5, payback. The cost basis is balco.nyc’s mid-tier planning figure of $1,200 for 800 W ($850 budget, $1,600 premium), hardware only, with no electrical work. No federal credit is netted out: the residential clean energy credit (IRC 25D) ended with 2025. Simple payback is cost over first-year savings. The calculator’s figure comes from a loop, in nominal dollars with no discounting:

  1. Year 1 savings = kWh × price.
  2. Each later year, output falls 0.5% (degradation) and the price rises 3% (the default; 2% and 4% are presets).
  3. Add each year to a running total until it reaches the kit cost, then interpolate within that year.

For the open New York panel, year 1 saves 603.9 × $0.34 = $205.33. The next four years save $210.43, $215.66, $221.02 and $226.51, for $1,078.96 after five. Year 6 adds $232.14, and $1,200 is reached 52% of the way through it: 5.5 years, against 5.8 simple.

Step 6, carbon. CO₂ avoided = kWh × the EPA eGRID2023 total output emission rate for the address’s grid subregion: 0.704 lb per kWh for WECC Southwest (Phoenix), 0.911 for RFC West (Chicago) and 0.864 for NYC/Westchester. eGRID’s non-baseload rates are higher in all three, so this is the conservative choice. Grid mix is why the Chicago panel avoids slightly more CO₂ than the Phoenix one (562 against 548 lb a year) while making 21% fewer kWh.

The total is 226.8 kWh. Three things follow:

  • New York pays back fastest while making the fewest kWh: 5.5 years open against Phoenix’s 9.0, and 13.3 shaded against 18.9. Price does that, not sun.
  • The choice of rate is worth a year. The same New York panel pays back in 5.5 years at Con Edison’s marginal rate and 6.6 at the state average. A calculator that does not say which it used has hidden a result-changing input.
  • Heavy shade in a cheap-power state can eat most of a panel’s life. Chicago’s shaded case needs 21 years on the loop. That argues for running your own address, not for giving up: a higher floor, a lower building or a wider street changes the answer quickly.

6The five numbers to check in any balcony solar calculator

A trustworthy balcony calculator shows five things. If any is missing, you cannot tell whether its answer is generous, cautious or wrong:

  1. Annual kWh, with the tilt and direction behind it. balco.nyc’s runs put an open 800 W vertical south panel at about 600–780 kWh across three very different cities. Near 1,000 kWh for a railing panel in the Northeast is probably a 35° rooftop number.
  2. The electricity rate, and whether it is marginal. Supply-only, average and all-in marginal rates answer different questions, and move the same New York panel’s payback by a year or more.
  3. The shading method. None, a flat percentage, or a horizon from real or described buildings, ideally applied by month. “No shading” on a low city floor is a best case, not an estimate.
  4. The self-consumption assumption. Whether every kWh is assumed to be used at home, and what surplus earns. Unless your state or utility credits exports, it earns nothing.
  5. The cost basis. Which kit price, whether wiring is included, and whether a tax credit was subtracted. The federal 25D credit ended with 2025.

balco.nyc states each of these with its estimates, and the methodology documents every constant used above. To run your own address, start at the calculator. To share an estimate, or have an assistant hand you one, use a prefilled link:

https://balco.nyc/?address=STREET+ADDRESS,+CITY,+STATE&floor=3&facing=south&across=tall&distance=street

facing takes the eight compass points; across takes none, low, mid, tall or tower; distance takes alley, street, wide or far. Optional tilt, watts, bill and rate (cents per kWh from your bill) also work. Outside New York City, a link with address, floor, facing and across runs at once; in the city you still pin your balcony on the 3D model, because real buildings replace the description. State figures are on the Arizona, Illinois and New York pages, and does my balcony get enough sun? and what balcony solar costs go deeper on shade and kit prices.

7Common questions

How much electricity will an 800 W balcony solar panel produce in a year?

It depends mostly on tilt and shade. balco.nyc's PVWatts runs put an 800 W south-facing panel hung vertically on a railing at about 778 kWh a year in Phoenix, 616 in Chicago and 604 in New York City with an open view, after a 5% railing loss. From a third floor facing a six-to-ten-story building across a 20 m street, the same panel makes about 328, 239 and 227 kWh.

How do I calculate balcony solar output by hand?

Take PVWatts' monthly output for your location, tilt and direction, multiply each month by 0.95 for a vertical railing mount and by that month's shade factor for the buildings opposite, and add the twelve months. PVWatts is NREL's free production model. The shade factor is the share of beam and diffuse light that still reaches the panel, and it equals 1.00 for an open view.

Why are balcony solar estimates lower than rooftop solar estimates?

Because a railing panel usually hangs vertically. In balco.nyc's PVWatts runs for Phoenix, Chicago and New York City, a vertical south-facing panel makes 60% to 64% of what the same panel tilted to 35 degrees would make, before any shading or railing loss. An estimate near 1,000 kWh a year for an 800 W railing panel in the Northeast is probably a tilted-panel figure.

What electricity rate should I use to calculate balcony solar savings?

Use the marginal rate: what one fewer kWh takes off your bill, including supply, delivery, surcharges and tax but excluding fixed monthly charges. To get it, subtract the fixed charges from a recent bill and divide by the kWh billed. In New York City balco.nyc uses Con Edison's all-in marginal rate of 34 cents; elsewhere it uses the EIA state average, which includes fixed charges and can overstate the value of an avoided kWh.

How much does a building across the street reduce balcony solar output?

It can more than halve it. In balco.nyc's model, a third-floor south-facing balcony facing a six-to-ten-story building 20 m away loses 58% to 62% of its annual output in Phoenix, Chicago and New York City. Most of the loss comes in winter, when the sun stays below the building opposite all day, and that is also when a vertical panel would produce the most.

How is balcony solar payback calculated?

Divide the kit cost by the first year's savings for a simple payback, or run a year-by-year loop in which output falls 0.5% and the electricity price rises 3% each year until cumulative savings reach the kit cost. For an unshaded 800 W vertical south panel in New York City at a $1,200 kit cost and Con Edison's 34-cent rate, balco.nyc gets 5.5 years on the loop and 5.8 years simple.

balco.nyc is an independent, free balcony-solar calculator: it estimates what a plug-in solar panel would produce and save at any US address, with a 3D shade model for New York City buildings. Not affiliated with the City of New York, Con Edison, NYSERDA, NREL, the EIA, the EPA, or any seller of solar equipment. NYC results carry a modeled uncertainty range of about 15%.
