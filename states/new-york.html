1Is plug-in solar legal in New York?

A plug-in solar bill (S8512C / A9111C) has passed New York’s legislature and awaits the Governor’s signature. Until it is signed and takes effect, the utility’s approval is still required.

In New York today, a balcony panel that feeds a household circuit is treated like any other generator. Con Edison asks anyone installing distributed generation to complete an interconnection application in its PowerClerk portal before installing, a process built for permanent systems. The SUNNY Act would replace that, for devices of up to 1,200 W AC, with a simple form sent to the utility within 30 days of installation. It has passed both chambers and awaits the Governor's signature; if signed, it takes effect 90 days later.

Two details decide how much it would change for an apartment. The bill requires a device to be listed or certified by an accredited laboratory as a complete plug-in system, and as of August 2026 no kit had been certified as a complete system under UL 3700, the standard written for that purpose. And it has no landlord, co-op, condominium or local-government provision, so a lease, a proprietary lease or a condo board’s rules on railings and facades would still decide whether a panel goes up. Exported power would be unpaid unless the customer signs a net-metering or other compensation agreement.

For New York City addresses, balco.nyc’s calculator models shade from surrounding buildings in 3D and values each kWh at Con Edison’s marginal rate of about 34¢, not the statewide average used elsewhere on this page. Where the SUNNY Act stands.

A9111C passed the Assembly 89-47 on 5/28/2026 and the Senate the same day (substituted for S8512C). As of 2026-09-23, neither chamber's action list shows delivery to the governor; the last action is 5/28/2026 'returned to Assembly'. That means the governor's decision clock has not started. The bill stays alive only through the end of the 2025-2026 term (Dec 31, 2026). If signed, it takes effect on the 90th day after becoming law (bill sec. 5). The sponsor memo says 'effective immediately', which conflicts with the text; the text governs. Devices of up to 1,200 W aggregate AC inverter nameplate would be exempt from interconnection and net-metering requirements. Exports would be uncompensated unless the customer voluntarily signs a net-metering or other compensation agreement. Utilities could not require approval, fees or extra equipment. Owners would have to notify their utility within 30 days via an online form. The bill has no landlord, HOA or local-government provisions.

3What it would save, and when it pays back

New York’s average residential price over the twelve months to June 2026 was 28.0¢/kWh, above the US average of 17.9¢, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration. That is an average: it spreads fixed monthly charges across every kWh, so it can differ from what a kWh you no longer buy actually saves. The calculator lets you enter the rate from your own bill instead.

In New York City the calculator values a kWh differently: at Con Edison’s all-in marginal rate of about 34¢, which excludes the fixed monthly charge a panel cannot reduce. At that rate the same south-facing panel would save about $205 a year. Why the two numbers differ; where the SUNNY Act stands.

Payback assumes every kWh is used at home, a 3% annual rise in the electricity price and 0.5% a year of panel degradation, with no discounting. With the $1,200 mid-range kit an east- or west-facing balcony pays back in 9.0 years and a north-facing one in 20.2 years. A household using New York’s average of 571 kWh a month would cover about 9% of its electricity with a south-facing panel.

4Emissions

New York City is on EPA’s NPCC NYC/Westchester grid subregion (NYCW), which emitted 0.864 lb of CO₂ per kWh generated in 2023 (eGRID2023). A south-facing panel producing 604 kWh a year would avoid about 522 lb of CO₂.

5Common questions

Is balcony solar legal in New York?

A plug-in solar bill (S8512C / A9111C) has passed New York’s legislature and awaits the Governor’s signature. Until it is signed and takes effect, the utility’s approval is still required. SUNNY Act (S8512C/A9111C) passed both houses May 28, 2026 but has not been delivered to Gov. Hochul. Would exempt plug-in solar up to 1,200 W AC from interconnection.

How much would a balcony solar panel produce in New York?

About 604 kWh a year for an unshaded 800 W system on a south-facing railing in New York City, 434 kWh facing east or west, and 167 kWh facing north, in balco.nyc’s PVWatts model. Shade from buildings or trees opposite lowers it; the calculator models that for your address.

How much would balcony solar save in New York?

About $169 a year for a south-facing panel at New York’s average residential price of 28.0¢/kWh, with a payback of about 6.6 years on a $1,200 kit. Savings assume all the power is used at home.

Does New York pay for surplus balcony solar power?

Surplus power would earn nothing: the bill excludes these devices from net metering.

Do renters in New York need permission to install balcony solar?

The law gives no right to install. A renter still needs the landlord’s permission, and a condo or co-op resident the board’s.

Can I use a balcony solar panel in New York City before the SUNNY Act is signed?

Only through Con Edison's interconnection process, which asks anyone installing distributed generation to apply through its PowerClerk portal before installing and was built for permanent systems. The SUNNY Act (S8512C / A9111C) would exempt plug-in devices of up to 1,200 W AC; it has passed both chambers and awaits the Governor's signature.
