Methodology

How balco.nyc calculates balcony solar production in NYC

balco.nyc estimates how much electricity, money, and CO₂ a plug-in solar panel could produce on any NYC balcony. The model combines NREL's PVWatts hourly simulation, a 3D shade model of the surrounding block built from NYC building footprints and street-tree records, an hourly self-consumption model of an NYC apartment, and Con Edison residential rates. Production is expected to land within about 15 to 25% of a real installation for a typical apartment; savings carry a wider band, because they depend on how much of the electricity is used as it is produced. Both are modeled bands, not field-validated ones. This page documents every assumption, source, and formula, and is the single canonical description of what the model does. The reasons behind each choice, the alternatives that were rejected, and the open review items are kept in the repository's docs/modeling-decisions.md, which does not restate the model.

1Energy production model

Annual production is computed by NREL's PVWatts V8 API, which simulates 8,760 hourly performance values across a typical meteorological year using the NSRDB satellite weather dataset. When PVWatts cannot be reached, a table of PVWatts results for New York City, computed in advance with the same parameters, takes its place (§1.2). There is no longer a separate hand-fitted fallback formula.

1.1 Primary model: NREL PVWatts V8

PVWatts is the US Department of Energy's reference solar production model. balco.nyc calls it with parameters tuned for a one- or two-panel plug-in kit on an urban balcony:

Soiling parameter robustness. NREL documents array parameters as pipe-delimited. If the parameter is rejected the entire request fails, which would silently drop every estimate onto the offline table while the interface still claimed a live simulation. The client therefore degrades explicitly: the pipe-delimited array first, then, if PVWatts rejects it, a second request with the array's annual mean folded into losses (10.6% for a vertical panel, 11.5% for a tilted one). A bracketed JSON form that used to sit between the two was dropped because the API answers it with HTTP 422 every time. Whichever variant succeeded is recorded on the result and named in the "What this estimate used" notice. A transport failure stops the sequence after one attempt, so an outage does not triple the load on the API.

PVWatts outputs used:

  • ac_monthly: 12 values of monthly AC energy (kWh), used for the production chart and multiplied by the per-month shade factors.
  • ac_annual: total annual AC output (kWh), shown as the unshaded figure in the breakdown.

Losses decomposition

PVWatts' default 14.08% is the compound of soiling 2%, shading 3%, snow 0%, mismatch 2%, wiring 2%, connections 0.5%, light-induced degradation 1.5%, nameplate 1%, age 0%, and availability 3%. Soiling is sent as its own monthly array and shading is the subject of Section 2, so both are removed from the bundle; what remains compounds to 9.6%, rounded to 9. A plug-in kit's one or two modules on a single micro-inverter carry less mismatch and wiring loss than a rooftop string, but a plug-in device is also unplugged, tripped and left unmonitored more often, so the availability term is kept. There is no separate "balcony loss adder": the differences between a rooftop and a balcony show up in the soiling array, the railing factor (§2.8) and the shade factor.

1.2 Offline PVWatts table (when the API is unreachable)

When PVWatts cannot be reached (network failure, rate limit, or no API key), production comes from js/pvwatts-nyc-table.js: PVWatts V8 (release 8.5.0) run in advance for 1 kW DC at every tilt and compass azimuth the calculator offers, at the NYC reference point (40.7128°N, 73.996°W; the NSRDB cell used is centred 2.3 km away at 40.73°N, 73.98°W, PSM V3 TMY-2020), with exactly the parameters above and dc_ac_ratio 1.0. The table is regenerated by scripts/build-pvwatts-table.mjs whenever a PVWatts parameter changes, and the raw grid is kept in docs/data/pvwatts-v8-nyc-grid-2026-09-03.csv.

Intermediate azimuths are interpolated linearly between the two neighbouring compass points on energy, and the twelve monthly shares are blended the same way so the seasonal shape follows the yield. Clipping. The table assumes a matched inverter. For an array larger than its inverter, a clipping loss measured from PVWatts hourly runs for NYC is interpolated on the DC:AC ratio: zero at or below 1.1, then for a vertical panel 0.3% at 1.2, 1.8% at 1.5 and 7.9% at 2.0; for a tilted panel 0.1%, 2.4% and 12.8%. Vertical panels rarely reach the inverter cap; tilted ones do on clear summer days.

Cross-check. The European Commission's PVGIS (release 5.2, PVGIS-NSRDB radiation database, 2005 to 2015) gives 972 kWh/kWp for a 1 kWp vertical south-facing crystalline-silicon system at the same coordinates with a 9% system loss, and 1,431 kWh/kWp at 35°; this table gives 937 and 1,433. The 4% gap at 90° is within the year-to-year spread of the weather.

1.3 Final energy formula

final_kwh = Σ ac_monthly[i] × shade_factor[i] × railing_factor                  (live PVWatts)
final_kwh = Σ unshaded_kwh × monthly_share[i] × shade_factor[i] × railing_factor   (offline table)

The monthly shade factors (Section 2) and the tilt-dependent railing factor (§2.8) are the only multipliers applied after PVWatts, on both paths. The self-consumption fraction (§4.3) is applied to savings, never to production.

2Shade model

PVWatts assumes an unobstructed installation. An NYC balcony faces buildings across the street, wings of its own building, the balcony slab above it and street trees, none of which PVWatts can see. The calculator therefore computes twelve monthly shade factors from a horizon model of the balcony's actual surroundings and multiplies PVWatts' monthly output by them. The same model runs in three ways: on the footprints and trees around a resolved address, either in the 3D view or headlessly when WebGL is unavailable; and, when an address has no footprint data, on a canonical street canyon that matches the visitor's description of the block (§2.6). There is no longer a static lookup band.

What this factor must and must not measure. PVWatts is given the panel's real tilt and azimuth, so it already prices orientation: it knows a north-facing vertical panel yields a fraction of a south-facing one. The shade factor is a second multiplier on that output, so it must answer only "how much of what PVWatts assumed actually reaches this balcony". Anything else is counted twice.

An earlier version charged every hour when the sun sat behind the facade as shading, crediting those hours only a fixed 30% diffuse share. That penalised orientation a second time: an entirely unobstructed east-facing balcony scored 0.54, and a north-facing one 0.32, with no neighbouring building present at all. The current model returns 1.00 for an unobstructed balcony at every orientation, and the test suite asserts exactly that.

2.1 Where the panel is

The balcony point is placed from the building's own geometry rather than from a fraction of its height.

  • Storey height = roof height ÷ number of floors, kept between 2.7 and 4.5 m (3.0 m when either figure is missing).
  • Panel centre height = ground elevation + (floor − 1) × storey height + 0.8 m. A first-floor panel sits 0.8 m above the ground slab and a top-floor panel one storey below the roofline, where the parapet and the neighbours still count. The earlier floor ÷ total floors × height placed every panel one storey too high, and a top-floor panel at the roofline with nothing left to obstruct it.
  • Horizontal position: on the facade whose outward normal best matches the chosen direction, at the point you clicked projected onto that wall (clamped to the middle 90% of its length), or at the wall's midpoint on the manual path, then pushed 1.5 m outside the facade line to where a panel hangs.
  • Floor from a click = floor(height above ground ÷ storey height) + 1, capped at the building's floor count. The floor can be corrected in the Customize panel.

2.2 Horizon profile

Every footprint within range, including the visitor's own building (so its wings and courtyards count), is projected onto a 360-bin azimuth skyline as seen from the panel. Each polygon edge is subdivided finely enough that no bin is skipped (every 2 m, and at least every 0.5° of angular extent), and each bin keeps the highest obstruction altitude found in that direction. Sample points within 1 m of the panel (the wall it hangs on) and buildings that do not rise above the panel are skipped. Because the nearest sampled point in a direction wins, concave and L-shaped footprints resolve correctly, and buildings that straddle due north wrap without seams. Neighbours modelled at more than three times the median roof height and above 60 m are counted and disclosed in the result, because a footprint carries a single roof height and a setback tower shades a little less than a full-height block would.

2.3 The balcony slab above (mount type)

A panel that sits back from the railing looks up into the underside of the balcony above. The Customize panel asks how the panel is mounted, and the answer sets how far the slab's edge projects beyond the panel plane: 0 m when it hangs outside the railing (the default, no slab effect), 0.6 m when it stands on the balcony floor, 1.5 m when it is fixed to the back wall. With the slab's underside h = storey height − 0.8 m above the panel centre and the edge d beyond the panel, every direction with altitude above atan(h × cos Δaz ÷ d) is blocked, where Δaz is the angle between that direction and the balcony's facing. There is no slab on the top floor.

On a typical side street (§2.6), floor 3 of 6, facing south, the annual factor of a vertical panel is 0.38 hanging outside the railing, 0.35 standing on the floor and 0.17 on the back wall. A 35° panel, which looks upward, goes from 0.59 to 0.44 and 0.15. The mount type is also logged with each estimate and shown in the result.

2.4 Street trees

Street trees within 80 m come from NYC Parks' Forestry Tree Points (up to 400 points, full-structure trees only). Each is a crown sized from its trunk diameter (DBH, in inches): crown base 3 m, crown top min(22, 4 + 0.55 × DBH) m, crown radius min(8, 1.2 + 0.30 × DBH) m. Trees are written into a transmittance mask over azimuth and altitude rather than into the opaque skyline: 20% of the beam gets through a crown in leaf (May to October) and 60% through bare branches, so a bare tree in front of a south balcony still costs winter output. Sky openness is recomputed through the mask for each season. A 12 m street tree in front of a low side-street balcony roughly halves its factor; treating it as an opaque building would overstate that loss by about 40%.

2.5 Irradiance-weighted daylight sweep

For each month, two representative days (the 8th and the 23rd) are swept from sunrise to sunset in 10-minute steps (computeAnnualShadeProfile). For each step:

  1. Sun position: altitude and azimuth for that day of year from SunPosition.calculateDoy (Section 5).
  2. Weather weights: the mean direct-normal (DNI) and diffuse-horizontal (DHI) irradiance for that month and hour, and the month's ground albedo, from js/irradiance-nyc.js, a 12 × 24 table extracted from the same NSRDB typical year PVWatts uses for NYC (local standard time). A blocked hour therefore costs what that hour is worth in the NYC climate rather than in a clear-sky idealisation. The earlier proxy, sin(altitude)^0.75 split 60/40 between beam and diffuse, under-weighted low sun by about 1.8×, and low sun is exactly the light buildings block.
  3. Three components on the panel plane, with β the tilt, θ the angle of incidence for the panel's tilt and azimuth, and GHI = DNI × sin(altitude) + DHI: 
    beam   = DNI × max(0, cos θ)
sky    = DHI × (1 + cos β) / 2
ground = albedo × GHI × (1 − cos β) / 2
  4. What survives: the beam passes only if the sun sits above the skyline in its azimuth bin and below the slab limit, at the transmittance of any tree crown in the way; the sky component is scaled by the panel's sky openness; the ground component by the street's openness.
received = beam × T_beam + sky × sky_open + ground × ground_open
assumed  = beam + sky + ground
monthly shade factor = Σ received / Σ assumed   over both sample days, clamped to [0.02, 1.00]
annual shade factor  = the same ratio over the whole year, each month weighted by its days

Sky openness is the fraction of the panel's isotropic sky view that survives the skyline, the slab and the trees, computed by integrating cos θ × cos(altitude) over the visible hemisphere. Because θ is the angle of incidence on the panel, it is tilt-aware: a 35° panel sees a different share of sky than a vertical one. Street openness is the horizontal sky view from a point on the street 6 m in front of the balcony at 1 m height, through the same footprints, so ground-reflected light is derated by how much sky the street itself sees rather than by the panel's own skyline; it is what keeps a deep canyon from going to zero in winter. Hours when the sun is behind the facade contribute near-zero beam to both sums, so they neither reward nor penalise. The clamp floor of 0.02 replaces an earlier 0.10 that hid real winter values in deep canyons.

Physics-vs-display separation. The coloured shadow heatmap in the 3D scene uses display_score = min(1, physics_score × 1.8) for UI contrast only, and that scorer serves the heatmap alone. The energy path reads the horizon profile, the slab limit and the tree masks, never the display score.

2.7 Direct sun hours

The result shows the hours of direct sun on the panel face on the 15th of June and of December: the minutes in the same sweep when the beam reaches the panel with at least half its strength through any tree crown. It is derived from the same horizon as the energy model, so the number shown and the number used cannot disagree.

2.8 Railing obstruction

The railing, the mounting hardware and the panel's own frame clip the bottom edge of a rail-mounted panel. Footprints cannot see any of it and PVWatts assumes an unobstructed module, so it is applied as a separate tilt-dependent factor: 0.95 at 90°, 0.97 at 70°, 0.98 at 60°, 0.99 at 35°. Field reports for vertical mounts put the loss at 5 to 8%; we take the conservative end. It applies to both production paths. Since the balcony slab above is now modelled geometrically (§2.3), this factor stands for the railing alone.

3Building orientation detection

When the user enters an address, we query NYC Building Footprints for the polygon geometry and detect facade directions.

Algorithm:

  1. Extract exterior ring coordinates from the building footprint polygon.
  2. Project to local metres: multiply every longitude delta by cos(latitude). A degree of longitude at NYC's 40.7°N covers only ~76% of the ground distance a degree of latitude does, so raw degree deltas stretch east-west edges by roughly 1.3× and skew every bearing by up to 7°.
  3. Compute edge vectors (dx, dy) between consecutive vertices, in metres.
  4. Calculate each edge's compass bearing: atan2(dx, dy) converted to degrees.
  5. Compute perpendicular facade directions: edge_bearing ± 90°.
  6. Sort edges by length (longest edge = primary facade).
  7. Rank facade bearings by solar potential: S > SE/SW > E/W > NE/NW > N.
  8. Return the best solar-facing direction as the suggestion, both exact and snapped to the nearest 45° compass point.

What is returned. bestDirection is the best solar-facing wall anywhere on the building, snapped to a compass point for labels; bestDirectionExact is the same wall's true bearing to a tenth of a degree. primaryDirections is the pair of walls belonging to the longest edge, which is where most units in the building actually face; they differ on, say, a north-south tower whose only south-facing wall is its short end.

Exact azimuth in the 3D view. When you click a wall in the 3D scene the panel takes that wall's true outward bearing, and PVWatts and the shade model receive it unsnapped; a Manhattan avenue facade at 209° is modelled at 209°, not at 225°. Snapping to eight compass points is used only for labels and for the manual path, where the visitor picks a direction. The Customize panel can override the detected direction with any of the eight compass points.

Confidence is "high" when the longest edge exceeds 1.3× the longest roughly-perpendicular edge (at least 30° off the primary), and "medium" otherwise. Comparing against the simple second-longest edge made "high" almost unreachable, because in any rectangle the two longest edges are the parallel long walls and are near-identical in length.

Manhattan grid note. Manhattan's street grid runs ~29° east of true north. A building that "faces the avenue" actually faces ~209° (SSW) or ~29° (NE). The algorithm reads this from the actual polygon, not from grid assumptions.

4Financial model

4.1 Electricity rate

Con Edison SC-1 residential rate: $0.35/kWh all-in marginal rate (supply + delivery + GRT + sales tax, excluding the fixed customer charge) for 2026, at about 300 kWh a month. Sources: Con Edison's SC-1 tariff and monthly supply rates, projected forward by the 2026 to 2028 electric rate plan approved by the Public Service Commission in Case 25-E-0072. Con Ed's delivery charge steps up in June to September for usage above 250 kWh a month, so a summer kWh at the margin costs a little more than this single figure; the model uses one annual rate. At 35¢ the rate is roughly double the US residential average of 17.3¢ (EIA, 2025).

The marginal rate is the right number for solar offset: every kWh used at home replaces one the household would have bought. The customer charge ($21.00 a month from 1 February 2026) is intentionally excluded because solar cannot offset a flat fee.

4.2 Consumption inferred from the bill

billable           = max(0, monthly_bill − 21)          # strip the fixed customer charge
annual_consumption = max(1, billable / 0.35 × 12)

The default $120 bill implies 3,394 kWh a year (283 kWh a month), close to Con Edison's typical apartment. The bill input is clamped to $20 to $800.

4.3 Self-consumption: savings only on electricity used at home

Savings are earned only on electricity used at home as it is produced. Annual production far below annual consumption does not prevent export: at midday on a weekday an apartment often draws a refrigerator and standby loads (100 to 300 W) against 400 to 600 W from the panel, and the surplus flows to the grid. Under the SUNNY Act (§4.7) that exported energy is uncompensated unless the owner separately enters a net-metering agreement, and Con Edison's meters record delivered and received energy separately, so every exported kWh is worth nothing. Earlier versions valued 100% of production, which overstated the default savings by about 8% and a weekday-empty apartment's by about a third.

Method. The share of production used on site was simulated hour by hour: the DOE/OpenEI residential load profile for New York Central Park (TMY3, 8,760 hours), reduced to an apartment archetype (interior lighting, appliances, plug loads and window air-conditioning; the single-family file's electric water heater, furnace fans and exterior lighting removed) and scaled to the household's annual consumption, against PVWatts V8 hourly AC output for six panel orientations at the same site (1,296 runs, in docs/data/self-consumption-sim-2026-09-03.csv). Three occupancy patterns were simulated, each rescaled to the same annual total so only the shape of the day changes: out most weekdays (the base profile), someone home (150 W added on weekdays 09:00 to 17:00) and nobody home on weekdays (weekday daytime load capped at a 120 W refrigerator-and-standby floor). The results were reduced to curves of on-site use against r = annual production ÷ annual consumption, one per orientation class (vertical south, vertical south-west, vertical east or north, vertical west, and 35° or 60° south) and occupancy, stored in js/self-consumption-table.js and interpolated at run time. Beyond the simulated range (r up to 1.1 for a vertical panel, 1.7 for a tilted one) the curve is extended along its last segment and never below 15%, since even a very oversized array covers the daytime base load.

At the same ratio a tilted panel scores higher than a vertical one because its summer-heavy output coincides with air-conditioning; at the same wattage it scores lower because it produces more. For the default case (800 W vertical south on an 800 W inverter, a $120 bill, a typical side street) r is about 0.2 and 85% of production is used at home. Who is home in the day moves savings more than any other single input, which is why the Customize panel asks.

self_consumed_kwh = annual_kwh × f(r, orientation class, occupancy)
exported_kwh      = annual_kwh − self_consumed_kwh              # shown in the result; earns nothing
annual_savings    = self_consumed_kwh × 0.35
monthly_savings   = annual_savings / 12
bill_offset_%     = self_consumed_kwh / annual_consumption × 100   # capped at 100%

Battery. The same simulation ran with 1 and 2 kWh of usable storage (90% round trip, 800 W charge and discharge). At the default ratio a 1 kWh battery lifts the used share by about 11 points, roughly 70 to 80 kWh or $25 to $30 a year for an 800 W panel, so a $600 battery increment takes more than 20 years to pay back at NYC rates; only an oversized array (r above 0.5) changes that. The result states what a 1 kWh battery would add as information. No battery is assumed.

Limitations. The load and PV series come from different typical years, so the coincidence of hot sunny afternoons with air-conditioning is not preserved. The load archetype is a scaled single-family shape, not a measured NYC apartment; a commuter-shaped synthetic profile gives 71% where the archetype gives 92% for the same household, so occupancy is the dominant uncertainty in the savings figure.

4.4 Payback period

Simple payback:

simple_payback = adjusted_cost / annual_savings

Escalated payback runs inside the same 25-year loop as the lifetime value (below): each year's savings compound the rate escalation and the panel's degradation, the inverter replacement (§4.5) is charged in its year, and the crossover year is interpolated for a fractional result. It applies no discount rate, so it is a nominal figure, not a net present value; it was once labelled "NPV payback", which overstated its rigour. If the cumulative balance never turns positive within 25 years the result shows "25+ yrs".

4.5 25-year lifetime value

year_savings[i]  = annual_savings × (1 − degradation)^i × (1 + escalation)^i     i = 0..24
lifetime_savings = Σ year_savings[i]                                     # nominal dollars
lifetime_net     = lifetime_savings − inverter_replacement
in today's $     = Σ year_savings[i] / 1.025^i − inverter_replacement / 1.025^12

Where:

  • degradation is tier-aware and system-level (the connectors, cabling and micro-inverter age along with the module), so it sits above module datasheet rates: premium 0.4%/yr, mid 0.6%/yr (the default), budget 0.9%/yr, leaving about 91%, 87% and 80% of original output in year 25. Sources: NREL/TP-5K00-88769 (Deline et al. 2024, fleet median about 0.75%/yr with module-level rates of 0.35 to 0.55%) and Jordan et al. 2022 (NREL/CP-5K00-81314, median 0.75%/yr, 90th percentile 1.9%). An earlier version cited a community-solar report by mistake.
  • escalation is user-selectable, default 3%/yr (mid), with low (2%) and high (4%) presets in the Customize panel. Long-run national EIA data tracks about 2 to 2.5%/yr; recent Con Ed history is closer to 7%/yr but skewed by one-off settlements. 3% is a reasonable central estimate; the band conveys honest uncertainty.
  • inverter replacement: kit micro-inverters carry 10 to 12 year warranties, so one replacement at 30% of the kit cost is charged in year 12 of both the payback and the lifetime figures.
  • today's dollars: the nominal total is restated at a 2.5% general inflation rate, because at 3% escalation the nominal 25-year total runs about 40% above what the same dollars buy today. Both figures are shown.

4.7 Offset assumption and regulatory status

Production offsets household purchases only to the extent it is used on site (§4.3); the remainder is exported and earns nothing.

Regulatory status (as of 3 September 2026). NY's SUNNY Act (S8512/A9111) exempts compliant plug-in devices up to 1,200W AC from interconnection and net-metering requirements. It passed the Senate unanimously in April 2026 and the Assembly on 28 May 2026, and awaits the Governor's signature; it takes effect 90 days after signing, so the realistic opening is early 2027.

Three consequences for this model: exported energy earns nothing under the Act unless the owner voluntarily enters a net-metering agreement, which is why savings count only self-consumed energy; a device must be listed by a nationally recognised testing laboratory as a complete plug-in photovoltaic system, and no kit carried that listing as of September 2026; and the Act grants no right to install, it removes the utility barrier only, so a landlord or co-op board can still refuse. The payback figures are conditional on a permission the model does not represent.

5Sun position (NOAA simplified algorithm)

js/sun-position.js implements a simplified NOAA solar-position algorithm tuned for NYC.

  • Day of year: the shade sweep (§2.5) uses the 8th and the 23rd of each month through calculateDoy(dayOfYear, minute); the sun arc in the 3D view and the direct-sun-hours figure use the 15th through calculate(month, minute).
  • Year is read dynamically from new Date().getFullYear() so the Julian-day base advances with time instead of drifting.
  • Daylight saving is computed from the calendar for the current year, the second Sunday in March to the first Sunday in November: in 2026 EDT (UTC−4) applies from day 67 to day 304 (8 March to 31 October) and EST (UTC−5) otherwise. The irradiance table is in standard time, so the sweep shifts clock time back an hour in summer before reading it.
  • Azimuth is computed via atan2(sin_az, cos_az), robust at all altitudes, with no separate acos branch. With the sine and cosine terms as defined, this already returns azimuth measured clockwise from north, and no further rotation is applied.

getDayBoundsDoy(dayOfYear) searches for sunrise and sunset by scanning altitude crossings, used by the shade sweep to bound its sampling loop.

A fixed sign error, and why it is tested now. Until 31 August 2026 this function added a further 180° after the atan2, which inverted every azimuth: June solar noon reported the sun as due north, and the morning sun as due west. The error fed the 3D scene lighting, the sun arc and the shadow model alike, and was invisible to inspection because altitude, and therefore sunrise, sunset and day length, stayed correct throughout.

The regression suite pins June and December solar noon to the south, checks peak altitudes against NOAA reference values, and asserts that azimuth sweeps monotonically eastward through the day.

6Environmental impact

CO₂ offset

co2_lbs = annual_kwh × 0.86

0.86 lbs CO₂/kWh is the EPA eGRID2023 output emission rate for the NYCW subregion (864.5 lb/MWh; released 2025, the latest edition), applied to all production, exported or not, since every kWh displaces grid generation somewhere. It is the "average grid" figure. EPA's own equivalencies calculator uses the non-baseload rate for avoided emissions (976.9 lb/MWh, or 0.98 lb/kWh, for NYCW); the average is the conservative choice, and because NYCW generation is 98% gas the two differ by only 13%. The figure is a year-one number: it does not follow the grid as it decarbonises over 25 years. Earlier versions carried the eGRID2022 value (885.2 lb/MWh) labelled as 2023.

Equivalencies

All three factors are from the EPA Greenhouse Gas Equivalencies Calculator (2024 references):

  • Trees: co2_lbs / 132, EPA's urban tree, 0.060 metric ton CO₂ per tree per year (a medium-growth deciduous tree sequestering 38 lb of carbon a year over its first ten years). Earlier versions used a 48 lb figure attributed to EPA that was the Arbor Day Foundation's.
  • Driving miles offset: co2_lbs / 0.88, EPA's typical passenger vehicle at about 400 g CO₂ per mile. Because this no longer equals the grid factor, miles and kWh are no longer the same number.
  • Smartphone charges: annual_kwh / 0.019, EPA's 2024 figure of 19 Wh per full charge, including the energy to hold the charge for the rest of the day.

7Data pipeline

7.1 Address resolution

  1. Google Places Autocomplete: user types address, gets type-ahead suggestions bounded to NYC (40.48°N to 40.92°N, 74.26°W to 73.70°W).
  2. On selection, extract lat/lon, formatted address, and address components. If Places is unavailable the typed address is geocoded on submit; if that fails too, a message says so and the manual entry panel opens.

7.2 Building data lookup

NYC Geoclient runs first through the site's own proxy (/api/geoclient, which holds the key, answers only balco.nyc pages and allows 60 requests per visitor per hour), because its BBL and BIN feed the other queries. The rest then fire concurrently via Promise.allSettled():

a) NYC Geoclient → PLUTO

  • Parse address into houseNumber, street, borough (with a corrected USPS ZIP atlas: prefix 104 → Bronx; Long Island 110 is intentionally excluded as it isn't NYC).
  • Call NYC Geoclient, get BBL and BIN.
  • Query PLUTO by BBL, get numfloors, yearbuilt, bldgclass, unitsres, bldgarea, zonedist1.

b) NYC Building Footprints (dataset 5zhs-2jue)

  • Query by BIN. Without a BIN, or when it finds nothing, take the footprint that contains the geocoded point (intersects(the_geom, POINT)); failing that, the nearest footprint within 30 m. Which of the three was used is recorded, and the proximity case is disclosed in the result.
  • Get the polygon, heightroof, groundelev; run orientation detection (Section 3).

c) Neighbour footprints, in three tiers, each an intersects query against a circle polygon:

  • every building within 200 m; buildings over 150 ft within 500 m; buildings over 350 ft within 1,500 m;
  • up to 1,500 footprints per tier, merged by BIN. A tier that returns its full 1,500 is flagged and the result says distant buildings may be missing.
  • The earlier single within_circle query was a containment test on polygon geometry, so it dropped every footprint that crossed the circle, which are the largest and tallest buildings (the Empire State Building was dropped from 150 m away).

d) Street trees: Forestry Tree Points within 80 m, up to 400.

The page waits for (c) and (d) before opening the 3D scene. The scene draws the buildings within 200 m; the shade model uses every fetched footprint and tree.

e) Floors: PLUTO numfloors; otherwise the roof height divided by 10 ft; otherwise 20. The result discloses which was used.

7.3 Form pre-fill

  • Manual entry panel: total floors and the suggested direction from the footprint; a hint shows where the floor count came from.
  • Building info card: address, floors, height.
  • In the 3D view the direction comes from the wall you click (its exact bearing) and the floor from the click height (§2.1); both can be corrected in the Customize panel.

7.5 Energy calculation

  1. Place the panel (§2.1) and compute the twelve monthly shade factors from the footprints and trees, or from the canonical canyon when there are none.
  2. Map the inputs to PVWatts parameters and call PVWatts V8; on failure, read the offline table.
  3. Multiply each month by its shade factor and by the railing factor.
  4. Infer consumption from the bill, look up the self-consumption fraction, and split production into used and exported energy.
  5. Run the financial model on the used energy with the selected tier and escalation preset, including the inverter replacement and the today's-dollars restatement.
  6. Compute environmental impact on all production.

7.6 Graceful degradation

Every API has a fallback, and every result carries a notice headed "What this estimate used" above the breakdown. It always names the production source, the shade source (with the number of buildings and trees), the floor-count source when it was estimated, and the self-consumption share; when a substitution was made it is marked as degraded.

The manual entry panel is the keyboard- and screen-reader-accessible route to a result; a button under the 3D scene opens it at any time. When footprints exist it runs the same horizon model headlessly, so the accessible path and the 3D path produce the same numbers.

7.7 Estimate logging

Each completed estimate is logged to the site's database for analytics on which paths visitors reach and what the model produces. The record holds the building's public lot and building identifiers (BBL and BIN), coordinates rounded to three decimals (about 100 m), and the inputs and results: azimuth, tilt, system and inverter watts, floor and floor count, the shading description when it was used, the shade source with its building and tree counts, mount type, occupancy, annual kWh, annual savings, self-consumption share, exported kWh, shade factor, whether PVWatts answered and with which variant, the data-source flags, and the calculator version. The street address is not stored, and the disclosure sits under the address box. The database grant is insert-only from the page.

9Accuracy & limitations

Expected accuracy. Production should land within about 15 to 25% of a real installation for a typical NYC apartment. The single figure hides several terms of different character, so the budget is stated term by term:

TermTypical sizeNotes
Weather year±5 to 8%A typical meteorological year against any single real year
Orientation±1% in the 3D view, ±3% when snapped to a compass pointThe 3D view uses the wall's exact bearing
Shade geometry±5% on open sites to ±15% in dense blocksFootprint heights carry no setbacks or parapets; awnings, AC units and signage are invisible; tree crowns are allometric estimates
Kit losses±3%The 9% bundle against a specific inverter and cable run
Placement on the balcony±3 to 5%The railing factor is a fixed derate; the slab depth is a three-way choice
Self-consumption (savings only)±10 to 25 points of the used shareWho is home dominates; the load archetype is not a measured NYC apartment

The offline table carries a slightly wider band than a live PVWatts run because it is computed at the city centre rather than at the address, though NYC's NSRDB cells differ by only a few percent.

This band is modeled, not measured. It is derived from the uncertainty of the inputs, not from comparing predictions against metered NYC installations. No such comparison has been run, and until the SUNNY Act takes effect there are no legal installations to meter. Treat it as a considered estimate of our own uncertainty rather than a validated tolerance, and read the cross-checks below as consistency checks against other models rather than against reality.

What the model captures:

  • Latitude-specific solar resource and seasonal variation (PVWatts NSRDB), including a vertical panel's winter-heavy year
  • Vertical and near-vertical tilt production, from PVWatts itself rather than from fitted factors
  • Azimuth to a tenth of a degree in the 3D view
  • Urban soiling at the level vertical glass actually collects (1 to 3% a month) rather than a rooftop profile
  • Inverter clipping for arrays larger than their inverter
  • A full horizon-profile model of every footprint that matters, including tall buildings up to 1.5 km away and the visitor's own building
  • Street trees with seasonal transmittance
  • The balcony slab above, by mount type
  • Isotropic diffuse sky and ground-reflected light, each reduced by its own openness
  • Weather-weighted sampling from the NYC typical year, so a blocked winter noon costs what it is worth
  • Tilt-aware angle of incidence in both the energy and shade models
  • Concave and L-shaped neighbouring footprints, and buildings straddling due north
  • Railing and mounting-hardware obstruction of the panel's lower edge
  • The share of production actually used at home, by orientation and occupancy, and the exported remainder
  • Tier-aware degradation, an inverter replacement, rate-escalation bands, and a today's-dollars total in the 25-year projection

What the model still does NOT capture:

  • Micro-shading from things in no city dataset: awnings, AC units, signage, a neighbour's plants
  • Setbacks and parapets: each footprint carries one roof height (setback towers are flagged, not reshaped)
  • Snow coverage in winter months (a vertical panel sheds quickly but not instantly)
  • Future electricity-rate trajectory, the single largest swing factor in lifetime savings (2% vs 4% escalation ≈ ±15% on lifetime $)
  • The household's real load profile: an archetype scaled to the bill stands in for it
  • Bifacial panels and light-painted walls (bifaciality is fixed at 0)
  • Time-of-use rate structure; a vertical panel shifts output toward winter and the shoulders of the day
  • Grid decarbonisation over the 25 years (the CO₂ factor is a year-one figure)
  • Kit prices for New York specifically (see D26 in docs/modeling-decisions.md)

Regression suite. tests/ exercises the shipped model files directly under Node (npm test, no dependencies). It pins solar position against NOAA reference values, asserts the invariant that an unobstructed balcony scores 1.00 at every orientation, checks horizon-profile geometry against concave and seam-straddling footprints, runs the PVWatts path against a recorded API response, checks the offline table against the live API within a few percent, and locks the shade, self-consumption, financial and environmental arithmetic and the consistency of the figures quoted across this page, the FAQ, its schema and llms.txt. Defects that were invisible to inspection, an azimuth sign error, a self-shading double-count, a one-storey placement error and a containment-only neighbour query, are covered by failing-first tests.

Cross-checks against other models:

  • PVGIS 5.2 (PVGIS-NSRDB, 9% loss) for NYC: 972 kWh/kWp vertical south and 1,431 at 35° south, against 937 and 1,433 in the offline table (§1.2).
  • NYSERDA NY Solar Map baseline: 1,238 kWh/kW/yr at optimal tilt with a standard module and default losses; this table's 1,433 at 35° south reflects the premium module class and the 9% bundle, with soiling and shading carried separately.
  • The offline table against a live PVWatts response for the same orientation, within a few percent, as a standing test.
