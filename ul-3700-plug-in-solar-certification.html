UL 3700 and whole-system certification for plug-in solar, explained

UL 3700 is the first US safety document written for a plug-in solar system as a whole, with panel, microinverter, cord and plug evaluated together. As of 23 September 2026 no complete plug-in system is listed to it. Only components, mainly microinverters, carry UL 3700 claims. balco.nyc read the certification clause in all nine signed state laws: only 2, Maine and Vermont, ask for a listing of the whole system, and they hold 0.6% of US residents. New York’s SUNNY Act, which has passed both chambers and awaits the Governor's signature, would require a “complete plug-in photovoltaic system” listing that no product holds.

1What UL 3700 is, and what it is not

UL 3700’s full name is the Outline of Investigation for Interactive Plug-In PV (PIPV) Equipment and Systems. Edition 1 is dated 11 December 2025 and is sold through the standards store of UL Standards & Engagement (ULSE), labeled a UL LLC outline. UL Solutions opened certification against it on 8 January 2026.

The word “outline” matters. ULSE’s glossary defines an outline of investigation as a certification document owned by UL LLC, not a consensus document and not developed through ULSE’s standards process. It is a certifier’s document for a category too new for a full standard: a normal first step, and what Maine, Vermont and New Jersey cite, but not an established national standard.

Its scope, as ULSE publishes it, is plug-in PV equipment and systems that connect by a plug-in PV cord and plug to a permanently installed receptacle meant for that purpose; Intertek, which also certifies to UL 3700, calls it a dedicated plug-in PV receptacle. The DC side is capped at 64 V open-circuit under the outline’s test conditions, and rooftop, building-integrated and hard-wired PV are excluded. The aim is the hazards that only exist once panel, microinverter, cord and household circuit are combined: current the breaker cannot see, plug blades that stay live, interference with ground-fault protection, and current flowing backward through devices built for one direction.

The case for it is a finding UL Solutions has made public. Its vice president of principal engineering, Ken Boyce, told PV Tech in August 2026 that the company bought plug-in products on the open market, tested them against UL 3700, and none complied. Whether that matters for your purchase depends on your state; balco.nyc’s compilation of every signed law’s certification wording is in section 4.

2UL 1741, IEEE 1547, NRTL and “listed”: the vocabulary

Four documents come up, and they answer different questions:

DocumentWhat it evaluatesWhat it does not tell you
UL 3700 (outline, 2025)Plug-in PV equipment, or a complete plug-in system with its cord, plug and protectionAnything not submitted to it: a microinverter evaluated to it is not a system evaluated to it
UL 1741 (ed. 3, ANSI)Inverters and other grid-interconnection equipment; used with IEEE 1547 and 1547.1 for grid-connected unitsThe panels, the cord and plug, or the circuit it feeds
IEEE 1547-2018How a generator interconnects with the grid, including islandingFire and shock safety of the product
UL 61730The PV module (panel)Everything downstream of the panel

Islanding is why utilities care: a generator that keeps energizing a line after the grid fails can shock a worker who believes it is dead. Most state laws add their own anti-islanding clause; Maine’s requires inverters that shut off 0.2 seconds after power is disrupted.

The legal vocabulary comes from federal workplace-safety rules. A nationally recognized testing laboratory (NRTL), under 29 CFR 1910.7, is an OSHA-recognized organization that tests for safety, lists or labels equipment, inspects production and is independent of the manufacturers it tests. OSHA’s current list has 21, including UL LLC, CSA Group, Intertek and both TÜV organizations. Every law below accepts an equivalent NRTL, not only UL, except Colorado’s 2027 sales rule, which says “UL labeled and listed”.

  • Listed (29 CFR 1910.399): named in a list published by an NRTL that inspects production, as meeting recognized standards or tested safe for use in a specified manner. Labeled: carrying that NRTL’s mark.
  • Certified is not defined there. UL uses “Certified (Listed)” interchangeably for end products; the state laws mix “certified”, “listed or certified” and “labeled and listed” without distinguishing them.
  • Recognized component (the backward “UR” mark): a part evaluated for use inside a larger certified product. UL says it is not a certification of a standalone product.

3Why a listed inverter and listed panels are not a listed plug-in system

A listing covers the product submitted, in the configuration tested, for a specified use. A microinverter listed to UL 1741 has been evaluated for its own safety and its behavior on the grid. A panel listed to UL 61730 has been evaluated for shock and fire safety as a panel. Neither was tested with the other, with the kit’s cord and plug, or feeding a shared 15 A kitchen circuit. Put them in one box and you have two listings, not one.

The hazards UL 3700 targets belong to the combination. An inverter feeding a circuit at one outlet while appliances draw at others can push more current through part of the wire than the breaker sees; balco.nyc’s worst case is 25 A on a 15 A circuit. No test of the inverter alone can find that. Live plug blades and GFCI interaction also depend on how the parts are connected.

The market shows the distinction. Bright Saver, a nonprofit that sells plug-in kits at cost, describes the microinverter in its kit as UL 3700 compliant. The same page says it cannot ship to New York, Maine or Vermont because their laws require “whole-system certification, which no system on the market has achieved yet.” In New York the law in question is the SUNNY Act’s text, which is not yet law. The kit ships to the rest of the continental US, which is how the market reads the other statutes: where a law names no system standard, a kit of listed components is sold as compliant. No regulator or court has tested that reading.

The count. Of the nine signed laws, three name UL 3700: Maine, Vermont and New Jersey. Only two, Maine and Vermont, ask for UL 3700 or an equivalent listing of the system itself, with no route through a combination of other standards. Five name no standard (Utah, Maryland, Colorado, Virginia, New Hampshire), and Connecticut names only the inverter standard. That is six of nine whose wording a kit of separately listed parts can plausibly meet, two that require a system listing, and New Jersey in between.

Weighted by population (Census Bureau 2025 estimates), Maine (1,414,874) and Vermont (644,663) hold 2,059,537 people: 0.6% of the 341.8 million US residents, and 5.0% of the 41.4 million in states with a signed law. If New York (20,002,427) and California (39,355,309) enact their bills, a system-level requirement would cover 61,417,273 people: 18.0% of US residents and 61% of everyone under one of these eleven texts. The missing product would go from a two-small-state problem to the main constraint for most people these laws cover.

5Where certification stands in September 2026

No complete plug-in system is listed to UL 3700 as of 23 September 2026. In late August, PV Tech reported that UL Solutions was still working toward its first UL 3700 certification. On 23 September, Bright Saver’s kit page still said no system on the market had achieved whole-system certification.

What exists are equipment-level claims for microinverters. On 15 July 2026 APsystems said its EZ1 plug-in microinverter was certified to UL 3700 by CSA Group, an NRTL on OSHA’s list. The same day Hoymiles launched its HiFlow Pro as the first UL 3700-compliant plug-in microinverter in the US; the coverage named no certifying lab. Neither claim is a listing of a complete system with panels, cabling and mounting, which is what Maine, Vermont and New York’s bill describe.

Two limits: balco.nyc could not search UL’s Product iQ directory, which requires an account, and has not searched every NRTL’s listings. If a complete system is listed after the review date above, the certifier’s directory is the authority, not this page.

For New York the timing is the point. The SUNNY Act has passed both chambers and awaits the Governor's signature. If signed, it takes effect 90 days later, and its interconnection exemption would cover only devices listed as complete plug-in systems; on today’s market, none. It grants no right to install, so renters and co-op residents still need permission (what the Act does and does not change).

6How to check a listing, and what “UL certified” can mean

A seller’s “UL certified” is a claim about some part of the product, to some standard. Four checks make it verifiable:

  1. Find the mark and file number on each part: the inverter’s nameplate and the panel’s label, not the box or the product page.
  2. Look it up with the certifier. UL’s Product iQ is free with an account and searches by file number, category code or model; CSA Group’s product listing is public. By the federal definition, a listed product is one that appears in such a list.
  3. Read what the listing covers: which standard, which model numbers, and whether it is the kit as sold or one part of it.
  4. Get it in writing and keep it. Ask the seller which lab listed which product, to which standard, under which file number. Maryland has you give the utility a certification of safety features, and Virginia tenants must show their landlord documentation.

7Common questions

What is UL 3700?

UL 3700 is the Outline of Investigation for Interactive Plug-In PV (PIPV) Equipment and Systems, edition 1, dated 11 December 2025. It is a UL LLC certification document rather than a consensus standard, and UL Solutions began certifying to it on 8 January 2026. It evaluates plug-in solar equipment or a complete plug-in system, including the cord, plug, receptacle arrangement and ground-fault and overcurrent behavior, rather than the panel or inverter alone.

Is any balcony solar kit certified to UL 3700?

Not as a complete system, as of 23 September 2026. Some microinverters carry UL 3700 claims: APsystems says its EZ1 was certified by CSA Group in July 2026, and Hoymiles launched its HiFlow Pro as UL 3700-compliant. But no complete kit with panels, cabling and mounting is listed to UL 3700. PV Tech reported in August 2026 that UL Solutions was still working toward its first UL 3700 certification, and Bright Saver says no system on the market has achieved whole-system certification.

If my kit has a UL 1741 microinverter and UL-listed panels, is it UL certified?

Its parts are, but the kit as a whole is not. A listing covers the product submitted, in the configuration tested. UL 1741 covers the inverter's safety and grid behavior, and UL 61730 covers the panel. Neither tests the combination with the cord, plug and household circuit, which is where plug-in hazards such as unseen overcurrent arise. Whether separately listed parts satisfy a particular state law depends on that law's wording.

Which states require UL 3700 for plug-in solar?

Of the nine states with signed plug-in solar laws as of 23 September 2026, Maine, Vermont and New Jersey name UL 3700. Maine and Vermont ask for UL 3700 or an equivalent listing of the system itself. Maine also allows a system configured to the state-adopted electrical code. New Jersey also accepts a comparable standard or combination of standards. Utah, Maryland, Colorado, Virginia and New Hampshire require NRTL certification without naming a standard, and Connecticut names UL 1741.

Does New York's SUNNY Act require UL 3700?

It does not name UL 3700, but it is stricter. The bill requires a device listed or certified by an accredited nationally recognized testing laboratory as a complete plug-in photovoltaic system. As of 23 September 2026 no complete system holds such a listing. The SUNNY Act has passed both chambers and awaits the Governor's signature; if signed, it takes effect 90 days later and grants no right to install.

What is an NRTL?

A nationally recognized testing laboratory is defined in federal rule 29 CFR 1910.7. It is an organization recognized by OSHA that tests equipment for safety, lists or labels it, inspects production and is independent of the manufacturers it tests. OSHA's list has 21, including UL LLC, CSA Group, Intertek and TÜV SÜD. State plug-in laws that say UL generally accept an equivalent NRTL as well.

How can I check whether a plug-in solar product is really listed?

Find the certification mark and file number on the nameplate of each part, then look it up in the certifier's own directory: UL's Product iQ (free with an account) or CSA Group's public product listing, for example. Check which standard the listing is to and whether it covers the complete kit or one component. A claim of being UL 3700 compliant is the maker's own statement, not a listing.
