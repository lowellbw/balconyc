Cost and payback

What does a balcony solar kit cost in NYC?

A balcony solar kit for a typical 800-watt New York City setup costs about $850 budget, $1,200 mid-range, or $1,600 premium; the nonprofit Bright Saver has sold a 360 W kit at cost since July 2026 for $414 plus a $29 membership. At Con Edison’s $0.34 per kilowatt-hour, a well-sited system saves roughly $300 a year and pays back in three to five years. A shaded, low-floor balcony takes six to ten.

1What a kit costs right now

balco.nyc models a typical 800 W New York City setup at three price points: $850 budget, $1,200 mid, $1,600 premium. That is panels, microinverter, cabling and a railing mount — the hardware, not the electrician.

Per watt, those work out to $1.06, $1.50 and $2.00. Per watt is the number to compare on, because a kit twice the size costs roughly twice as much and produces roughly twice as much, so kit size barely moves your payback period. Siting does.

For an external reference point on the low end: Bright Saver, a nonprofit, opened nationwide at-cost sales in July 2026 at $285.30 for 180 W and $414.17 for 360 W to members, against $499 and $699 without membership. Membership is $29 a year.

We cite this because it is the best-documented at-cost price in the US market and it tells you what the hardware genuinely costs before markup. It is not a recommendation. balco.nyc does not rank brands, take affiliate revenue, or tell you what to buy.

Prices in this market moved sharply during 2026. Any figure you read without a date on it is worthless. Every price on this page carries the date we found it.

2The costs that are not the kit

The kit price is the part that is easy to look up, which is why most coverage stops there. Budget for the rest before you decide:

  • An outlet. A plug-in system needs a weather-rated outlet on the balcony. Many NYC balconies do not have one. Adding one is a licensed-electrician job, often through an exterior wall, sometimes requiring building permission. We have no reliable price for this in NYC and will not guess — get two quotes before you buy a kit. On a balcony with no outlet this can be a large fraction of the kit price.
  • Mounting. Included brackets suit common railing profiles. Glass balustrades, thick concrete parapets and unusual rail geometry may need something else.
  • Permission. Co-op boards, condo boards and landlords may say no, or say yes with conditions on the mount, the colour, or the visibility from the street. That costs time, and occasionally a more expensive mount.
  • Membership or shipping, where a seller charges them.
  • Replacement. Panels and microinverters are usually warranted for different lengths of time. Check both before assuming a 25-year life.

These are excluded from the payback table below. Add yours to the kit cost column and re-read it.

3What a kilowatt-hour is actually worth to you

balco.nyc values avoided consumption at $0.34/kWh — Con Edison SC-1 all-in marginal cost, supply plus delivery plus surcharges. It is the number that changes when you use one less kilowatt-hour, and why we use it rather than a supply-only figure is a page of its own.

Your bill also carries a fixed monthly customer charge. Solar does not touch it. It is excluded from the $0.34 marginal rate, and in the worst siting case it is larger than everything the panels save you. Your bill will not go to zero.

The bigger point, and the one most coverage of balcony solar gets wrong: there is no net metering for these devices. The SUNNY Act exempts them from interconnection and net-metering requirements, which cuts both ways — no utility approval, and no credit for export. A kilowatt-hour your panels make while your apartment is drawing less than they produce goes to the grid and earns you nothing.

So every saving figure below is a ceiling: it assumes every kilowatt-hour generated displaces one you would otherwise have bought. Multiply by whatever share you actually self-consume. As arithmetic rather than as a balco.nyc estimate: if a fifth of your generation were exported, a $187 year-one saving becomes about $150, and a 6.0-year payback becomes about 7.4 years.

4The payback arithmetic, worked

An 800 W system in New York City produces roughly 400–900 kWh a year. The spread across balconies is about 3× from worst to best — high floor facing south against low floor facing north, with the buildings across the street doing most of the damage.

5How to read the 25-year number

The last column is in nominal dollars, not discounted to present value. This matters more than it sounds. Nominal totals are the standard way this gets reported and they systematically flatter the result.

Take the 550 kWh row. Twenty-five years of escalating, degrading output totals about $6,375 nominal. Discount that stream at 3% a year and it is worth about $4,277 in today’s money. Net of a $1,200 kit: +$5,175 nominal, +$3,077 in present value. Same panels, same sun, a third less money once you account for the fact that a dollar in 2051 is not a dollar now.

Two other assumptions sit inside that number. Rate escalation is set at 3%/year by default, with 2% and 4% presets — at 2% the totals fall noticeably and paybacks stretch, and this is a forecast, not a measurement. Panel degradation is 0.5%/year on the mid tier, so year-25 output is about 88% of year one.

And the model itself carries a stated accuracy of ±15%, widening to ±20% where it falls back to coarser irradiance data. That is a modelled band, not field-validated — nobody has yet put instrumented kits on a representative sample of New York balconies. A 6.0-year payback should be read as roughly 5.2 to 7.1 years. See methodology for how the band is derived.

6When the answer is no

If your balcony is on a low floor, faces north, or spends the middle of the day in the shadow of the building opposite, the arithmetic is bad and you should not spend the money on economic grounds.

Work it through. Roughly 400 kWh a year is $136 of avoided electricity at best. Put that against a $1,600 premium kit and payback is 10.4 years before you add an electrician, before you subtract exported kilowatt-hours you are not paid for, and before the ±15% band, which could push it past 12. If you may move within five years, and you probably will if you rent, you will not get your money back.

Buy the cheapest kit that is properly certified, or do not buy one. A budget kit on a poor balcony pays back in 5.9 years; a premium kit on the same balcony takes 10.4. Paying more for a system that will never see full sun is the single most expensive mistake available here.

None of this is legal in New York yet. The SUNNY Act passed both chambers in late May 2026 and awaits the Governor’s signature; it takes effect 90 days after signing. Do not buy hardware on the assumption the timeline will hold — see the SUNNY Act status page.

7Common questions

How much does a balcony solar kit cost in NYC?

For a typical 800 W setup, balco.nyc models $850 at the budget end, $1,200 mid-range and $1,600 premium — hardware only. Smaller kits cost proportionally less: the nonprofit Bright Saver has sold a 360 W kit at cost since July 2026 for $414 to members, plus a $29 annual membership. None of these figures include an electrician, and many NYC balconies need one to add an outdoor outlet.

Is balcony solar worth it in New York?

It depends almost entirely on your balcony, not on the kit. A high-floor, south-facing, unshaded balcony producing 900 kWh a year saves about $306 and pays back a mid-range kit in 3.8 years. A low-floor, north-facing one producing 400 kWh saves $136 and takes 8.1 years on the same kit — or over 10 on a premium one, which is a bad deal.

Do I get paid for the electricity I don't use?

No. The SUNNY Act exempts these devices from net metering, so any kilowatt-hour you generate and do not consume at that moment goes to the grid uncompensated. This is the most important fact about the economics and most coverage gets it wrong. Every saving figure on this page assumes full self-consumption and is therefore a ceiling, not an expectation.

What is the payback period for balcony solar in NYC?

Between roughly 2.7 and 10.4 years, depending on siting and kit price, assuming full self-consumption. A mid-range $1,200 kit lands between 3.8 and 8.1 years across the typical 400–900 kWh production band. Those figures include 3%/year rate escalation and 0.5%/year panel degradation, and carry a modelled plus or minus 15% accuracy band.

Will balcony solar zero out my Con Edison bill?

No. Con Edison charges a fixed monthly service charge that does not change with how much you generate. On a poorly sited balcony that fixed charge alone can exceed the entire annual saving. Solar only reduces the variable portion of the bill, which balco.nyc values at $0.34 per kilowatt-hour.

Should I buy the premium kit?

On a well-sited balcony the difference is about 1.2 years of payback and defensible. On a poorly sited one it is 4.5 years and hard to justify. Since output is set by your floor, orientation and shading rather than by kit quality, paying up for hardware that will never see full sun is the most expensive error available. balco.nyc does not recommend specific products or brands.
