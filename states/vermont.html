1Is plug-in solar legal in Vermont?

Vermont has a plug-in solar law in effect (S.202 (Act 149)), in force since 1 July 2026. Small plug-in systems no longer need the utility’s interconnection approval there, within the limits below.

Vermont’s law is in force, but in practice it is waiting on equipment. A qualifying device must comply with UL 3700, the standard written for complete plug-in systems, or hold an equivalent certification from another nationally recognized testing laboratory, and must run to IEEE 1547-2018. The first microinverter certified to UL 3700 was announced in July 2026, but as of August 2026 no kit had been certified as a complete system under the full standard, according to the Vermont Energy & Climate Action Network.

Once one is, the rules are unusually concrete. There is no utility approval, fee, interconnection agreement or notice, but a device may only be connected where there is a smart meter; Burlington Electric Department’s plan for about 20,000 smart meters was approved by the Public Utility Commission in 2012, and a customer without one cannot use this law. A household that already net meters cannot add a device, and exports earn nothing. A tenant must give the landlord at least 10 days’ notice; the landlord may reply within 10 days with reasonable restrictions, such as having a licensed electrician do any electrical work at the tenant’s cost, and if it does not reply the tenant may proceed. Deed restrictions and covenants, including those covering patio railings in condominiums, can no longer prohibit a plug-in device, and zoning bylaws may not regulate one.

The law also requires a device to be attached to the ground or a structure “temporarily but securely”; balco.nyc’s figures below assume a panel fixed to a balcony railing.

Under 30 V.S.A. sec. 256, a customer may install one or more plug-in devices per meter, up to 1,200 W combined inverter capacity. Devices may only connect to smart-metered systems and must be temporarily but securely attached. They are exempt from sec. 248 CPG, interconnection agreements and PUC jurisdiction. Utilities cannot require approval, installation fees or extra equipment, but may recover costs if the device overloads the service. Net-metering customers cannot also install one, the device is not eligible for net metering, and exports are expressly uncompensated. Tenants must give the landlord 10 days' notice. The landlord may impose reasonable restrictions (e.g. a licensed electrician at the tenant's cost); if the landlord does not respond, the tenant may proceed. Deed restrictions and covenants (including condo patio-railing rules) cannot prohibit plug-in devices, and zoning bylaws may not regulate them. No source conflicts found.

3What it would save, and when it pays back

Vermont’s average residential price over the twelve months to June 2026 was 23.6¢/kWh, above the US average of 17.9¢, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration. That is an average: it spreads fixed monthly charges across every kWh, so it can differ from what a kWh you no longer buy actually saves. The calculator lets you enter the rate from your own bill instead.

Payback assumes every kWh is used at home, a 3% annual rise in the electricity price and 0.5% a year of panel degradation, with no discounting. With the $1,200 mid-range kit an east- or west-facing balcony pays back in 9.5 years and a north-facing one in 18.5 years. A household using Vermont’s average of 574 kWh a month would cover about 10% of its electricity with a south-facing panel.

4Emissions

Burlington is on EPA’s NPCC New England grid subregion (NEWE), which emitted 0.539 lb of CO₂ per kWh generated in 2023 (eGRID2023). A south-facing panel producing 658 kWh a year would avoid about 355 lb of CO₂.

5Common questions

Is balcony solar legal in Vermont?

Vermont has a plug-in solar law in effect (S.202 (Act 149)), in force since 1 July 2026. Small plug-in systems no longer need the utility’s interconnection approval there, within the limits below. Act 149 (S.202), in force July 1, 2026: plug-in PV up to 1,200 W combined per meter, UL 3700, smart meter required, no interconnection, no export credit; renter, HOA and zoning protections.

How much would a balcony solar panel produce in Vermont?

About 658 kWh a year for an unshaded 800 W system on a south-facing railing in Burlington, 478 kWh facing east or west, and 219 kWh facing north, in balco.nyc’s PVWatts model. Shade from buildings or trees opposite lowers it; the calculator models that for your address.

How much would balcony solar save in Vermont?

About $156 a year for a south-facing panel at Vermont’s average residential price of 23.6¢/kWh, with a payback of about 7.1 years on a $1,200 kit. Savings assume all the power is used at home.

Does Vermont pay for surplus balcony solar power?

Surplus power earns nothing: the law excludes these devices from net metering.

Do renters in Vermont need permission to install balcony solar?

Yes, in part: the law limits a landlord’s or association’s power to refuse. The details are in the notes above.

Can a landlord in Vermont stop a tenant installing balcony solar?

Not outright. Under 30 V.S.A. 256 a tenant gives the landlord at least 10 days' notice; the landlord may respond within 10 days with reasonable restrictions, such as requiring the tenant to pay for any electrical work by a licensed electrician. If the landlord does not respond, the tenant may proceed. A tenant may not do electrical work on the premises without the landlord's permission.

Do I need a smart meter for balcony solar in Vermont?

Yes. Act 149 allows plug-in photovoltaic devices to be connected only to systems using smart meters, and only up to 1,200 W of combined inverter capacity per meter.
